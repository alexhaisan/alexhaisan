## 1. The key distinction

> *"**Social preferences** refer to how people rank different allocations of material payoffs
> to themselves and others. Self-interested individuals care only about their own material
> payoffs."* — Camerer and Fehr (2002, p. 2, §1, ¶1)

Two distinct non-selfish motives, and the difference is examinable:

| | **Altruism** | **Reciprocity** |
|---|---|---|
| Definition (Camerer and Fehr, 2002, p. 2, §1, ¶2) | *"an actor takes costly actions to increase the payoff of another actor, **irrespective of the other actor's previous actions**"* | *"people are willing to reward friendly actions and to punish hostile actions **although the reward or punishment causes a net reduction in the material payoff of those who reward or punish**"* |
| In one word | **Unconditional** kindness | **Conditional** on what the other did |
| Measured by | Dictator Game | Ultimatum, Trust, Public Good with punishment |

> ⚠️ Note Camerer and Fehr distinguish this from **reciprocal altruism** in biology, which
> *"typically assumes that reciprocation yields a net increase in the material payoff"*
> (p. 2). Here the punisher is genuinely worse off. That is what makes it a deviation.

## 2. The seven games (Camerer and Fehr, 2002, Table 1, pp. 7–8)

| Game | Prediction with rational, selfish players | Experimental regularity | Interpretation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Prisoners' dilemma** | Defect | **50% choose Cooperate.** Communication increases cooperation. *(Dawes 1980)* | Reciprocate expected cooperation |
| **Public Goods** | Each contributes nothing, gᵢ = 0 | **Contribute 50% of endowment** one-shot. Contributions unravel over time; majority gives 0 in final period. Communication strongly increases cooperation. Punishment greatly increases contributions. *(Ledyard 1995)* | Reciprocate expected cooperation |
| **Ultimatum** | Offer ε, the smallest money unit; any x>0 accepted | **Most offers between 0.3S and 0.5S. Offers < 0.2S rejected half the time.** *(Güth et al 1982)* | Responders punish unfair offers; negative reciprocity |
| **Dictator** | No sharing, x = 0 | **Proposers allocate ≈ 0.2S** on average; strong variation across experiments and individuals *(Kahneman et al 1986)* | **Pure altruism** |
| **Trust** | Trustee repays nothing; investor invests nothing | **y ≈ 0.5S; trustees repay slightly less than 0.5S**; repayment increasing in y *(Berg et al 1995)* | Trustees show positive reciprocity |
| **Gift Exchange** | Worker chooses minimum effort; employer pays minimum wage | Effort increases with the wage; employers pay far above the minimum *(Fehr et al 1993)* | Workers reciprocate generous wage offers |
| **Third Party Punishment** | A allocates nothing; C never punishes A | Punishment of A is higher the less A allocates to B *(Fehr and Fischbacher 2001a)* | C sanctions violation of a sharing norm |

## 3. The Public Goods Game in detail

### Structure (L3 s11)
4 players · 10 tokens each · simultaneous choice of how much to invest · experimenter
**doubles** the pool · pool shared **equally** · payoff = tokens kept + share of pool.

Formally (Camerer and Fehr, 2002, p. 9): payoff πᵢ = y − gᵢ + mG, where m < 1 < mn.
Each pound contributed costs you £1 but returns only m < 1 privately — so **self-interest
predicts zero**. But mn > 1, so **the group as a whole gains**. That gap is the dilemma.

### The empirical pattern
| Finding | Source |
|---|---|
| One-shot contributions average **40–60%** of endowment; distribution is **bimodal** — most people give everything or nothing | Camerer and Fehr (2002, p. 10) |
| Contributions **decline substantially** over repeated periods | p. 10 |
| **60–80% contribute nothing in the final period** | p. 11 |
| About **half** are **"conditional cooperators"** — they contribute more when they expect others to | p. 11 *(Croson 1999; Fischbacher, Gächter and Fehr)* |
| About **a third** are purely self-interested and never contribute | p. 11 |

> **Why contributions decay** (p. 11): not because people learn to free-ride, but because
> *"reciprocal subjects gradually notice that they are matched with free-riders and refuse to
> be taken advantage of."* Andreoni (1988) showed a surprise **restart** sends contributions
> back up — which would not happen if people had simply learnt the selfish strategy.

### Punishment — Fehr and Gächter (2000)
Two stages: the public goods game, then each player may assign up to **10 punishment
points** to any other. One point cuts the target's first-stage income by **10%** and **also
costs the punisher**.

Self-interest predicts **zero punishment**, and therefore no difference in contributions.

> *"The experimental evidence **completely rejects** this prediction."*
> — Camerer and Fehr (2002, p. 12)

**Figure 1 (p. 12):** standard game falls from ~45% to ~15% over ten periods; when punishment
is introduced at period 11, contributions jump to ~65% and climb to **~95%**.

Two refinements worth knowing:
- *"the actual rate of punishment is very low in the last few periods — the mere **threat** of
  punishment… is enough"* (p. 13)
- The punishment pattern is almost identical in **partner** and **stranger** protocols, so
  *"the strategic motive of inducing future cooperation is **not** an important cause of the
  punishment"* (p. 13). People punish because they want to, not to build a reputation.

### Cross-cultural: antisocial punishment
From the supplied video (Joshua Greene), describing **Herrmann, Thöni and Gächter (2008)**,
*'Antisocial punishment across societies'*, *Science*, 319, pp. 1362–1367:

- Same game, 16 cities worldwide, identical rules and comprehension checks
- **Boston, Copenhagen, St Gallen** — cooperation starts high and stays high
- **Seoul** — starts mid, ramps up as punishment is enforced, ends like Copenhagen
- **Muscat, Athens** — starts low, stays low, never recovers
- The cause: **antisocial punishment** — *"the people who didn't cooperate were punishing the
  cooperators for cooperating"*
- Correlates with **World Values Survey** attitudes to tax evasion and fare-dodging

## 4. Conventions of experimental design (Camerer and Fehr, 2002, pp. 4–5)

Useful for the December essay's method critique — and it **answers** the "lack of realism"
objection from L2 slide 10 rather than conceding it:

> *"Experimental economists are usually interested initially in interactions among anonymous
> agents who play once, for real money, without communicating. **This stark situation is not
> used because it is lifelike (it's not). It is used as a benchmark** from which the effects
> of playing repeatedly, communicating, knowing who the other player is, and so forth, can be
> measured by comparison."* — p. 4

> *"abstract language is used **not because it is lifelike, but as a benchmark** against which
> the effects of concrete descriptions can be measured. It is well-known that there are
> **framing effects, or violations of the principle of description invariance**."* — p. 5

Other conventions named: the **taboo on deception** (p. 6) · **stranger** vs **partner**
protocols · **stationary replication**, the *"Groundhog Day"* design · the **strategy
method** · show-up fees of $3–5.

> **Andreoni (1995)** is cited at p. 5 for the result that *"players who are asked to **take**
> from a common pool for their private gain typically behave differently than subjects who
> are asked to **give** to the common pool"* — which is exactly the **Taking Game** on
> L4 slide 15.

## 5. How any of this connects back to IRC

| Component | What strategic games expose |
|---|---|
| **Action** | The option set includes actions that cost you money to hurt or help someone else — IRC says you'd never take them |
| **Preferences** | IRC's *thin* version allows social preferences; the *thick* version (self-interest) is what these games refute |
| **Expectations** | Conditional cooperators act on **beliefs about others' behaviour** — the Evidence→Expectations link under strategic uncertainty |
| **Instrumentality** | *"How those outcomes came to be is irrelevant"* (L3 s6). Reciprocity says the opposite: **intentions matter**. This is the deepest clash. |

> That last row is the strongest December essay angle. The Computer UG (L4 s12) isolates it:
> if the same unequal split is acceptable from a computer but not from a person, then what
> you object to is not the **outcome** but the **intention** — and instrumental rationality
> has no room for that.
