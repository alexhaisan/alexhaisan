## 1. What behavioural economics is

> *"…research and methods from economics and other social sciences with the goal of improving
> the **descriptive value** of economic theory"* — Weber and Dawes (2005), quoted L2 slide 7

⚠️ Citation not yet verified — see `../07-references/02-bibliography.md`.

Note the phrase **descriptive value**. This matches Schoemaker's purposes distinction
(Week 1 reading list): behavioural economics attacks IRC's *descriptive* adequacy, not its
*normative* standing.

### The research process (L2 slide 7)
```
identify anomaly → study using new method → generate new theory → apply theory
```

Croson and Gächter (2010, p. 124, §4, ¶4) describe the same cycle from the theory side:
experiments *"formulate new models consistent with robustly observed behavioral
regularities"*, and new theories are useful for three reasons — they organise the evidence
better (with the old theory as a special case), they generate new testable predictions, and
*"if and when the new theories are disproved, they provide a language to talk about the
results which they helped to organize."*

## 2. Experimental economics (L2 slide 8)

- Economists gather empirical data to systematically evaluate economic theories
- Traditionally they use **field data** — observing naturally occurring phenomena
- **Experimental economics** uses **laboratory** experiments to gather experimental data
- Lab experiments are created for scientific purposes; the environment is **artificial** and
  **under the control of the experimenter**
- Two disciplinary conventions:
  - **Incentive-compatible choices** (real monetary rewards)
  - **No deception**

> Croson and Gächter (2010, p. 124, §3, ¶1) define it more tightly:
> *"Experiments are a controlled data generating process. 'Control' means that most factors
> which influence behavior are held constant and only one factor of interest (the
> 'treatment') is varied at a time. This controlled variation of factors is crucial for being
> able to draw causal inferences."*
>
> And (p. 124, §3, ¶3): *"Like theoretical models, experiments are simplifications of the
> world. They are equally (or sometimes more) descriptively inaccurate than economic models…
> That said, just as descriptively inaccurate theories are useful, descriptively inaccurate
> experiments are useful as well."*

### Pros and cons (L2 slide 10)

| ✅ Pros | ❌ Cons |
|---|---|
| Not dependent on natural environment | **Lack of realism** — is the data representative of the real world? |
| Data generated in a short time | **"Dirty test tubes"** |
| **Sterile environment** — "noises" removed | **Demand effects** |
| Ability to vary key aspects and **isolate specific variables** | **Costly** |

> **"Dirty test tubes" comes from Binmore (1999)** — see `../05-further-reading/notes/binmore-1999.md`.
> It is worth knowing that the phrase is used by Binmore **against the experimentalists**,
> not against economic theory. Getting that the right way round is a cheap way to show you
> read the source rather than the slide.

## 3. Field experiments (L2 slide 12)

- Occupy a **middle ground** between lab experiments and naturally occurring field data
- **Trade-off between experimenter control and increased realism**
- Try to run a controlled experiment that captures important real-world characteristics
- Use **"non-standard" subjects** — participants from the market of interest, *"not Western
  UG students"*
- Use **field context** in the commodity, task, stakes, or information set — not induced
  valuations or abstract instructions

**Worked example: Gneezy and Rustichini (2000), 'A Fine Is a Price'.** Ten private day-care
centres in Haifa, 20 weeks, real parents, real money, real contract. The canonical field
experiment on this module's reading list — full notes at
`../05-further-reading/notes/gneezy-rustichini-2000.md`.

## 4. Surveys (L2 slide 15)

**Survey data** — quantitative data about items in a population (demographics, attitudes,
personality). Researcher-administered (interviews) or self-administered (questionnaires).

| ✅ Pros | ❌ Cons |
|---|---|
| Available, easy to administer, **cheap** | **Not incentivised** |
| **Cross-validation** of results from accepted methods such as experiments | **Hypothetical bias** |
| | **Framing and order effects** |
| | **Social desirability bias** |

Every one of those four cons is evidenced in **Bertrand and Mullainathan (2001)** — the
Week 2 additional reading that maps onto this slide exactly. Full notes with locators at
`../05-further-reading/notes/bertrand-mullainathan-2001.md`.

## 5. The methods compared

| | Lab experiment | Field experiment | Survey | Field (observational) data |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Control** | Highest | Medium | Low | Lowest |
| **Realism** | Lowest | Medium | Medium | Highest |
| **Causal inference** | Strong | Strong | Weak | Weak |
| **Incentives** | Real money | Real stakes | None | Real |
| **Subjects** | Often Western UG students | Market of interest | Population sample | Whoever is observed |
| **Cost** | High | High | Low | Low |

> Croson and Gächter (2010, p. 125, §5, ¶3) place experiments between theory and data:
> `Theory —— Experiment (Lab/Field) —— Observational data`
>
> And (p. 130, §7, commandment 1): *"All areas of science (economics included) need to be
> tolerant of multiple methodologies… Theory, lab experiments, field experiments, social
> experiments, neuroeconomics, observational research, simulations, surveys, case studies,
> etc., all these (and more) contribute to our understanding of the world."*

## 6. How to use this in the December essay

The essay asks you to *"use a theory or experiment which we have studied on this module to
explain your behaviour"*. Whichever experiment you pick, one or two sentences of **method
critique** will lift the piece:

| Move | Example sentence shape |
|---|---|
| Name the method | *"This is a field experiment, which trades experimenter control for realism (L2)."* |
| Note the limitation | *"As a lab study using undergraduate subjects, its external validity is open to question."* |
| Cite the critique | *"Binmore (1999, p. F17) argues economic theory should only be expected to predict where the problem is simple, incentives adequate and time for trial-and-error sufficient — conditions this design does not meet."* |
| Note the defence | *"Croson and Gächter (2010, p. 124) reply that descriptive inaccuracy no more disqualifies an experiment than it disqualifies a model."* |

That last pairing — a named critique and a named reply — is worth more than either alone.
