## Strategic games — the core claim (L3 s6 = L4 s4)

> - *"When outcome is dependent on another person – need to consider another person when
>   making choice"*
> - *"Instrumental rationality predicts that subjects are only concerned with their **own
>   utility** when making strategic choices (i.e. **no regard for the utility of the other
>   person**)"*
> - *"Results from these games elicit subject's **social preferences**"*
> - *"**Instrumental** implies subjects are only concerned with **outcomes** – how those
>   outcomes came to be is **irrelevant**"*

> 🔑 **That last bullet is the one to steal.** It is the sharpest statement of
> instrumentality in the whole module, and it is what exemplar 1 exploits: if only outcomes
> matter, then *how* a sum of money came to you cannot bear on how you should spend it.

### Game theory (L3 s7)
> *"Game Theory is a useful tool for **strategic interaction**, but it assumes **rational

> **dominant strategies** and **Nash equilibria**."*

## The games — rules, predictions, findings

Full reference with sources: [`../../10-strategic-games/01-games-and-social-preferences.md`](../../10-strategic-games/01-games-and-social-preferences.md)

| Game | IR prediction | Observed | Deviation indicates |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public Good** (L3 s11–12) | Contribute nothing — free-ride | 30–50% of endowment; decays with repetition | **Cooperation** |
| **Ultimatum** (L4 s6–8) | Proposer: smallest non-zero. Responder: accept anything | Offers 40–50%; reject below ~20% | **Fairness**, negative **reciprocity** |
| **Dictator** (L4 s13–14) | Give nothing | ~20% of stake | **Altruism**, unconditional kindness |
| **Trust** (L4 s16–17) | Send nothing / return nothing | Send 40–60%; return ≈ amount sent | **Trust**, **trustworthiness**, positive reciprocity |

### Public Good Game variations (L3 s12)
- Communication increases cooperation (**Sally 1995**)
- Punishment increases cooperation with each repetition, and subjects are **willing to punish
  free-riders at cost to themselves** (**Fehr and Gächter 2000**)
- Evidence: Marwell and Ames 1981; Isaac et al 1985; Andreoni 1988

### Ultimatum Game variants (L4 s10–12, s15)
| Variant | What it tests | Change to the rules |
|---|---|---|
| **Earnt UG** | Do **social norms** affect the outcome? | Proposer answers 10 general-knowledge questions; the stake = number correct |
| **Emotion UG** | Do **emotions** influence the Responder? | Responder may send a **message** before deciding ← **this is Xiao and Houser (2005)** |
| **Computer UG** | Do people care about **inequality itself**, or about intentions? | A computer picks the split at random from £9/£1 … £5/£5 |
| **Taking Game** | Does **framing** matter? | B *takes* from A rather than A *giving* to B. *"Framing of decisions has an impact, even when the situation is identical"* |

### Dictator Game findings (L4 s14)
- Dictators give ~20% (Forsythe et al 1994; Hoffman et al 1996; Eckel and Grossman 1996, 1998)
- Giving **falls** when decisions cannot be identified by the experimenter (Hoffman et al 1994)
- Giving **rises** when the receiver introduces himself briefly (Bohnet and Frey 1999)

### Social preference theories (L4 s9)
| Theory | Source | Claim |
|---|---|---|
| **Inequality aversion** | Fehr and Schmidt (1999) | *"People prefer higher payoffs but also prefer allocation of payoffs to be **equal**"* — **guilt versus envy** |
| **Reciprocity** | Rabin (1993), *"fairness equilibrium"* | *"The other player's actions and **intentions** affect whether a player reciprocates positively or negatively"* |

> The difference matters: inequality aversion is about **outcomes**; reciprocity is about
> **intentions**. The **Computer UG** (L4 s12) is designed to tell them apart — if you reject
> a 9:1 split chosen by a computer, it's the inequality; if you only reject it when a person
> chose it, it's the intention.
