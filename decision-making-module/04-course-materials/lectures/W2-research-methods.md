## §Component Mapping — the four questions (slide 4)

The lecturer's own framing of the mapping task:

| Component | The question to answer |
|---|---|
| **Action** | *What are the available choices in this decision scenario?* |
| **Preferences** | *What do I want/value in this choice? What do I want to avoid?* |
| **Expectations** | *What are the possible outcomes of each choice?* |
| **Evidence** | *What evidence am I using to form my expectation?* |

> Note the second half of the Preferences question — ***"What do I want to avoid?"*** None
> of the four exemplars answers it. It is a free way to make your Preferences row richer.

## 🔑 §Component Mapping — the pitfalls (slide 5)

**The most directly useful slide in either lecture.** Verbatim:

> - **Action:** *"usually fairly easy, but think about how choice is **restricted by framing**"*
> - **Preferences:** *"Avoid privileging some preferences over others, **'I went against my preferences' is not a compelling argument**"*
> - **Expectations:** *"Beware of **entangling expectations and preferences**, what are the outcomes of my decision?"*
> - **Evidence:** *"Often the relationship between expectations and evidence is important, **might be worth combining them**"*

### What each one is telling you

| Slide 5 line | What it means for your submission |
|---|---|
| Choice restricted by framing | The Action deviation is about options you never considered — frame the set wrongly and the rest follows |
| *"'I went against my preferences' is not a compelling argument"* | ⚠️ **This is the single most important warning in the module.** It rules out the most tempting answer. See below. |
| Don't entangle expectations and preferences | Keep "what I wanted" out of "what I expected" — and if they *did* mix, that **is** your deviation (wishful thinking) |
| Combining Evidence and Expectations is fine | A real word saving at 300 words; exemplars 3 and 4 do it |

> **On *"I went against my preferences"*:** you have conflicting preferences, so *every*
> choice goes against one of them. Saying you acted against a preference describes a
> trade-off, not a failure. The deviation has to be in **how** you traded off, or in the
> Action/Expectations/Evidence components — not in the bare fact that one preference lost.

> *"Look at one of the decision scenarios on the worksheet, and analyse it using Elster's IRC model"*
> 1. *"What maps to each component in this scenario?"*
> 2. *"How would a rational decision maker (homo economicus) have made this decision?"*

## §3 Experimental Economics (slides 8–10), Field Experiments (12), Surveys (15)

Captured in full, with the supporting literature, at
[`../../09-research-methods/01-methods-of-behavioural-economics.md`](../../09-research-methods/01-methods-of-behavioural-economics.md).

Headlines:
- Lab: artificial, experimenter-controlled, **incentive-compatible**, **no deception**
- Pros: control, speed, isolation of variables, *"noises removed"*
- Cons: realism, **"dirty test tubes"**, demand effects, cost
- Field experiments: middle ground; **non-standard subjects** (*"not Western UG students"*)
- Surveys: cheap and available, but **not incentivised**, hypothetical bias, framing/order
  effects, social desirability bias

## §Example decisions (slides 18–20)

Offered as coursework inspiration.

### Consumer behaviour (slide 18)
> *"IRC predicts consumers are aware of the consequences of their purchases. However, we see
> violations of this – e.g. consumers who say they care about the environment, but also buy
> fast fashion. **How does this fit with evidence/expectations?**"*

### Product image / branding (slide 19)
> *"IRC predicts humans will update expectations in accordance with evidence, so **only
> product information is relevant**. However, firms pour a huge amount of resources into
> product image & marketing. Why does this influence consumers?"*

### Other examples (slide 20)
Free-riding in teams · Stock market bubbles · Corporate culture · Consumer addictions ·
Mental accounting · Product pricing/menus · Subscription products

> Both worked examples locate the deviation at **Evidence→Expectations**. That is where the
> lecturer's attention is, and it is the component the exemplars handle least well.
