## §1 Introduction (slide 13)

- Economics: *"the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between **ends**
  and **scarce means** which have alternative uses"* (Robbins)
- **Choice** underpins economics — a **trade-off between alternatives**
- Economists study behaviour to **explain and predict** → to prescribe policy
- Models comprise **variables** and **assumptions**: `behaviour = f(opportunities, preferences)`
  - Variables: behaviour, preferences, beliefs
  - **Core assumption: behaviour or choice is rational**

## §2 Instrumental Rationality (slide 14)

- Rational choice theory: *"when faced with several courses of action, people usually do what
  they believe is likely to have the best overall outcome"* (**Elster 1989**)
- In economics rational choice is **instrumental**: individuals **only look at the outcome**
  when evaluating potential actions (utility maximisation).
  **"Ends always justify means (in fact, means don't matter at all)"**
- ***Homo economicus*** — agents behaving in an instrumentally-rational manner

## §2b The IRC decision procedure (slides 15 & 21 — shown twice)

1. List all available choices
2. Define goals / decision-making criteria
3. For each choice, assess all possible consequences against goals/criteria, and estimate the
   **utility** and the **probability** of that consequence
4. Calculate **expected utility** for that choice by multiplying utility by probability for
   each possible consequence
5. **Choose the outcome with the highest expected utility**

> Repeated on slide 21 at the start of the application section — a strong signal that this
> is the procedure to walk through when analysing your own decision.

## §3 Components of Rationality (slides 16–19)

**Elster 1989** — four components, built up cumulatively across the deck:

| Component | Definition given |
|---|---|
| **Action** | A choice between alternative courses of action |
| **Preferences** | Goals, desires, aims, wants (which give utility) |
| **Expectations** | Probabilities, beliefs |
| **Evidence** | Information, data |

### On models (slides 17 & 19 — also shown twice)

- Elster's IRC model is **a model** — a simplification/representation of how humans decide
- Intended to help us understand decision-making: what factors influence it? why was a
  particular choice made?
- *"The model is only useful to the extent that we can **map the components onto aspects of
  an actual decision-making scenario**"*
- *"The model is a simplification — **it doesn't always work well. This is useful**, as it
  helps us to understand how human decision-making deviates from the model"*

> That third bullet is effectively the mark scheme for the intermediate assessment: the
> exercise *is* mapping the components onto an actual scenario.

## §4 Application (slides 20–29)

**Worked example in class:** *"My daughter is 17, and wants to buy her first car. How does
Elster's model of rational choice map to her decision? What maps to Action, Preferences,
Expectations and Evidence in this scenario? What does the model predict about her decision?"*

### Action (slides 22–23)
- List all available **choices** in this decision
- **Requires very strong framing of the decision**
- *"Rational decision-makers never have 'I didn't think of that!' moments"*
- **Violations at this step often involve unconscious restriction of available possible choices**

### Preferences (slides 24–25)
- *"Reason is slave to the passions"* (David Hume)
- Used to **evaluate** actions; represent desires, goals, things to avoid
- *What am I trying to achieve through this decision? How do I evaluate different possible outcomes?*
- Preferences are often **contradictory**, and involve **trade-off** — *"this is an important
  element of the analysis"*
- 🚨 **"Preferences cannot be intrinsically irrational"**
- 🚨 **"Watch out for confusion about the word 'irrational' here!"**

### Expectations (slides 26–27)
- What are the **possible outcomes** of each action, and how likely is each?
- Also called **beliefs** — *"what do you believe will be the outcome of each action.
  Not religious beliefs!"*
- 🚨 **"Expectations should be formed without any impact from preferences — no wishful thinking"**
- Expectations are usually formed on the basis of **evidence**; violations occur when this
  process is done incorrectly, or when other factors influence it

### Evidence (slides 28–29)
- Everything a rational decision-maker uses to **construct expectations**
- Includes life experience, facts/knowledge learnt, **advice from others**, research,
  **observations of others' experience**
- Some decisions involve **evidence search** — this can be influenced by preferences and
  expectations
- Violations often noticeable **through impact on expectations** — evidence is
  **over- or under-valued**

## §5 Homework (slide 30)

> *"You need to practice using the IRC to analyse decisions. This is often harder than you
> would think! Over the next week, try and observe your decision-making process — try it with
> big decisions and small ones. Try and identify the different components of the model. Does
> your decision-making follow IRC? Where does it deviate?"*

**This is the intermediate assessment in miniature.** Do it.

## Workshop material (slides 8, 11–12)

Reflection activity: *"Tell your group about a decision you made recently. Was it a 'big' or
a 'small' decision? Can you describe how you made the decision? Do you think your
decision-making was rational? Did you make the right decision?"*

Class feedback captured on slide 12 — decision-making:
is a **process** · involves **trade-offs** · depends on **experience** · can be informed by
others · can be **conscious or unconscious** · involves thinking about **consequences** ·
can be difficult or easy · **isn't random** · **sometimes leads to a bad outcome**

> The last point is the lecturer pre-empting the outcome/process confusion. It aligns exactly
> with Elster p. 209: consequences cannot explain a decision.
