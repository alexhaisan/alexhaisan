## 1. Where IRC sits

**Economics** = *"the science which studies human behaviour as a relationship between ends
and scarce means which have alternative uses"* (Robbins, cited slide 13).

**Rational choice** = *"when faced with several courses of action, people usually do what
they believe is likely to have the best overall outcome"* (Elster 1989, cited slide 14).

**Instrumental** rational choice = individuals **only look at the outcome** when evaluating
potential actions. *"Ends always justify means (in fact, means don't matter at all)"*
(slide 14).

The agent who behaves this way is ***homo economicus*** — driven purely by outcome; ageless,
genderless, without historical, institutional or cultural identity.

### 🔑 The exam-style question: Rational Choice vs *Instrumental* Rational Choice

The lecturer asked you to pay attention to the word "instrumental". The answer is in
Hargreaves-Heap (1992, ch. 1), which distinguishes **three senses of rationality**:

| Sense | What is rational | Who chooses the ends? |
|---|---|---|
| **Instrumental** | The **means** best satisfying given ends | Nobody — ends are given |
| **Procedural** | Following a **rule or norm** | The rule/society |
| **Expressive** | Deliberating on and **creating the ends themselves** | The agent, reflexively |

So: *rational choice* is the broad family; ***instrumental*** rational choice is the narrow
member of it in which **reason serves ends it does not itself evaluate**. Hume's line, quoted
on slide 24: ***"Reason is slave to the passions."***

Hargreaves-Heap puts it exactly: *"Reason can assess only their **consistency**, not their
**content**."* That single sentence is the difference between the two terms, and it is the
root of the trap in §5 below.

## 2. The four components (Elster, Figure 11.1)

The lecture and the book use different labels for the same model. **Know both** — write with
the lecture's, and you can note the book's in parentheses to show you did the reading.

| Lecture (slide 16) | Elster's book | Definition |
|---|---|---|
| **ACTION** | Action | A choice between alternative courses of action |
| **PREFERENCES** | Desires | Goals, desires, aims, wants (which give utility) |
| **EXPECTATIONS** | Beliefs | Probabilities, beliefs |
| **EVIDENCE** | Information | Information, data |

### The structure

```
                        ACTION
                       ↗      ↖
                      ╱        ╲
            PREFERENCES ──╳──> EXPECTATIONS
            (desires)            (beliefs)
                      ╲          ↑  ╷
                       ╲         │  ↓
                        ╲        │  │
                         ↘    EVIDENCE ⟲
                           (information)
```

Read it as follows:

- **Preferences → Action** and **Expectations → Action**: the action is chosen on the basis
  of what you want and what you believe.
- **Evidence ⇄ Expectations**: evidence shapes beliefs; beliefs also shape how much further
  evidence it is worth gathering.
- **Preferences → Evidence**: how much information it is worth collecting depends on what
  you want. *"More obviously, it makes sense to gather more information before making an
  important decision such as buying a house than when choosing between two equally expensive
  bottles of wine"* (Elster, p. 205).
- **Evidence ⟲ (self-loop)**: *"The optimal search for information may also depend on the
  results of the search itself"* (p. 206) — the berry-picking example. If you find a rich
  patch immediately, stop looking.
- **Preferences ─╳─> Expectations is BLOCKED.** This is the crossed line in Figure 11.1 and
  it is the most important single feature of the diagram. **What you want must not influence
  what you believe.** That is **wishful thinking**, and it is irrational by definition.

> **The subtlety worth stealing** (Elster, p. 205 n.12):
> *"Wishful thinking, in which 'the wish is the father of the thought,' is clearly
> irrational. By contrast, there is nothing irrational about the process shown in
> Figure 11.1, in which the desires are, as it were, the **grandfather** of the beliefs."*
>
> Desires may legitimately shape beliefs **through** evidence-gathering (I want this badly,
> so I research it thoroughly, so my beliefs improve). They may **not** shape beliefs
> **directly** (I want this badly, so I believe it will work out). Father = irrational.
> Grandfather = fine. Few students will spot this; it is a cheap, precise distinction to
> deploy.

### The arrows mean two things at once

> *"In Figure 11.1 the arrows have a double interpretation, in terms of **causality** as well
> as of **optimality**. The action, for instance, should be **caused by** the beliefs that
> make it a rational one; **it is not enough to do the right thing by fluke.**"* (p. 191)

So a correct action reached by accident is **not rational**. Elster's example: a smoker's
two opposing biases may exactly cancel, producing the same belief an unbiased observer
would hold — *"that does not prove they are rational."* He notes that Max Weber erred here,
inferring *"process rationality" from "outcome optimality"*: deviation from the rational
course is a **sufficient** condition for irrationality, but not a **necessary** one.

Likewise the stick-or-snake reflex: jumping back is **adaptive**, and mimics what rational
calculation would have produced, but is *"not rational in the strict sense, since it is not
produced by the machinery of rational decision making"* (p. 192).

## 3. The three optimality requirements

> *"An action is rational, in this scheme, if it meets **three optimality requirements**:
> the **action** must be optimal, given the beliefs; the **beliefs** must be as well
> supported as possible, given the evidence; and the **evidence** must result from an
> optimal investment in information gathering."* — Elster (2007, p. 191)

| | Requirement | Fails when |
|---|---|---|
| **(i)** | Action optimal, **given beliefs** | You believed correctly and acted otherwise |
| **(ii)** | Beliefs best supported, **given evidence** | You had the evidence and drew the wrong inference — or wished your way to a belief |
| **(iii)** | Evidence from **optimal investment** in gathering | You searched far too little, or far too much, for what the decision was worth |

This is the diagnostic. *"My decision satisfied (ii) and (iii) but violated (i)"* is a
precise, high-credit sentence — far better than "I was irrational."

> On (ii): *"Rational beliefs are those that are shaped by processing the available evidence
> using procedures that, in the long run and on average, are most likely to yield true
> beliefs"* (p. 202). Note it is only a **necessary** condition for rationality, not a
> sufficient one — Elster's example of someone who rationally sticks a pin in a voodoo
> puppet, *given* their belief about how killing works.

## 4. The IRC decision procedure (slides 15 & 21)

The lecture gives the model as an explicit five-step procedure. **This is almost certainly
the structure to follow when applying the model to your decision.**

1. **List all available choices**
2. **Define goals / decision-making criteria**
3. For each choice, **assess all possible consequences** against the goals/criteria, and
   estimate the **utility** and the **probability** of that consequence
4. **Calculate expected utility** for that choice — utility × probability, summed
5. **Choose the outcome with the highest expected utility**

$$EU(a) = \sum_i p_i \, u(x_i)$$

## 5. 🚨 The trap: preferences cannot be irrational

Slide 25, verbatim:

> *"Preferences **cannot** be intrinsically irrational."*
> *"Watch out for confusion about the word 'irrational' here!"*

The lecturer has flagged this explicitly, which means it is where students lose marks.
Elster is blunter (p. 193):

> *"There is no implication that the desires be **selfish**. **The confusion of rationality
> and egoism is a crude error**, although one that is facilitated by the practice of some
> rational-choice theorists."*

And, more surprisingly:

> *"Nor do we need to require that desires be **stable**, not even in the minimal sense of
> excluding temporary preference changes. **An agent who under the influence of emotion or
> drugs prefers A to B acts rationally in choosing A**, even if she under other circumstances
> prefers B to A."*

### What this rules out of your coursework

| ❌ Not a deviation | Why |
|---|---|
| "I wanted the enjoyable thing rather than the sensible thing" | A preference. Admissible. |
| "I was selfish" / "I was generous" | Content of preferences. Admissible — *"a crude error"* to call this irrational. |
| "I was angry/drunk/tired and wanted something different" | **Elster explicitly says this is still rational.** Temporary preference change is allowed. |
| "It turned out badly" | Outcome ≠ process. See §6. |
| "I didn't know enough" | A constraint — unless the *search* was non-optimal, which is (iii). |

### What counts as a genuine deviation

A failure located at one of the **four component sites**, as the lecture sets them out:

| Site | Lecture's description of violations |
|---|---|
| **ACTION** (slide 23) | *"Requires very strong framing of the decision. Rational decision-makers never have 'I didn't think of that!' moments. Violations at this step often involve **unconscious restriction of available possible choices**."* |
| **PREFERENCES** (slide 25) | Not irrational in themselves — but *"preferences are often **contradictory**, and involve **trade-off** — this is an important element of the analysis."* The deviation is in how conflicting preferences were resolved, not in having them. |
| **EXPECTATIONS** (slide 27) | *"Expectations should be formed without any impact from preferences — no **wishful thinking**. Violations occur when this process is done incorrectly, or when other factors influence it."* |
| **EVIDENCE** (slide 29) | *"Some decisions will involve evidence search — this can be influenced by preferences and expectations. Violations often noticeable through impact on expectations — evidence is **over- or under-valued**."* |

**Use these four sites as your checklist.** They are the module's own framework, in the
module's own words, and they map directly onto Elster's three optimality requirements.

## 6. Rationality is subjective through and through

Elster's closing section (pp. 209–212), which the lecturer flagged as among the most
important pages:

> *"**Consequences of a decision cannot explain it.** Only the mental states that precede the
> decision enable us to **explain** the actions as optimal from the point of view of the
> agent rather than to **characterize** them as useful or beneficial from the point of view
> of an external observer (**or of the agent at a later time**)."*

Two things follow, and both are directly usable:

1. **Outcome ≠ process.** A good decision can have a bad outcome. You are being asked about
   the decision *as made*, not as it turned out.
2. **Your later regret is not evidence of irrationality.** "Or of the agent at a later time"
   — the you of today is an external observer of the you who decided. If your only argument
   is "I regret it now", you have not yet shown a deviation.

> *"The **rationality** of beliefs is a completely different matter from that of their
> **truth**. Whereas truth is a feature of the relation between the belief and the world,
> rationality is a feature of the relation between the belief and the **evidence possessed by
> the agent**."* (p. 211)

A beautiful, highly quotable line — and the cleanest statement of why a belief that turned
out false can still have been rational.

### Two further ideas from the closing pages

- **Belief traps** (p. 211): a rational agent can be stuck with a **false** belief if the
  believed costs of testing it are too high. The myopic person whose myopia prevents him from
  finding his glasses is *"trapped"*. Also: *"many beliefs must be taken at face value
  secondhand, since if we were to test them all we would never get on with our lives."*
- **The discounting pill** (pp. 209–210): if a pill existed that would make you weight the
  future properly, a rational agent would **refuse** it — *"Any behavior that the pill would
  induce is already within my reach."* Likewise a selfish person refuses an "altruism pill".
  But footnote 16 concedes that under **hyperbolic** discounting an agent might rationally
  take it, as **precommitment** against weakness of will. A superb hook into the
  self-control material later in the module.

## 7. The limits of the model — for evaluation marks

The lecture is upfront (slides 17/19): *"The model is a simplification — it doesn't always
work well. **This is useful**, as it helps us to understand how human decision-making
deviates from the model."* Treat IRC as a **benchmark**, never as a target to demolish.

### Elster's own limits: indeterminacy (pp. 206–209)

Rational-choice theory can fail in **two** distinct ways:

1. **Indeterminacy** — it fails to yield a *unique* prediction
2. **Irrationality** — people fail to live up to its predictions (the next chapter's topic)

Sources of indeterminacy: indifference; **incomplete** preferences; uncertainty where
outcomes cannot be compared; and above all **the difficulty of knowing the optimal investment
in information gathering** — between a lower and upper bound on search time *"there may be a
large interval of indeterminacy"*, within which **no choice can be called irrational**.
Elster floats replacing "rationality" with ***non-irrationality***.

> Note: unlike intransitivity, **incompleteness is not a failure**. *"A lack of completeness
> is not any kind of failure... reflects simply a fact of life"* (p. 194). This differs from
> the standard textbook treatment and is worth knowing.

### Hargreaves-Heap's limits (1992, pp. 14–17)

- **Empirical:** EU maximisation yields predictions *"which have been consistently falsified"*
  experimentally. And many human projects are **states that are essentially by-products**
  (Elster) — you cannot *decide* to fall asleep or to be spontaneous. The strategy collapses
  the moment you notice you are pursuing it.
- **Contradictory desires / multiple selves:** Bismarck — *"Faust complained that he had two
  souls in his breast. I have a whole squabbling crowd."* Becker & Stigler (1977) try to
  reconcile apparent preference change with stable preferences; Hargreaves-Heap objects that
  their agents *"do not experience any inner conflict"* — they never weigh the pleasure of
  another drink against the regret in the morning.
- **Theoretical (the information regress):** investing in information until marginal benefit
  equals marginal cost **begs the question** — how would you know the marginal benefit
  without already having the information? *"From a point just below the summit of the
  foothills, there may seem little advantage in climbing to the top. Yet an aerial photograph
  would reveal that the tops of the mountains which lie beyond afford unimaginably better
  views."* Each answer needs a rationality-of-beliefs-about-beliefs answer behind it.
