## What I told you before

Based on Elster (2007, p. 193):

> *"Nor do we need to require that desires be **stable**, not even in the minimal sense of
> excluding temporary preference changes. An agent who under the influence of **emotion or
> drugs** prefers A to B **acts rationally in choosing A**, even if she under other
> circumstances prefers B to A."*

I concluded from this that "I was tired/stressed/emotional and wanted something different"
was **disqualifying** — not a deviation at all.

## What the lecture actually says

**Lecture 3, slide 16**, under the heading *Initial Assessment*:

> *"**Preferences** – Rarely irrational, but look for situations where **preferences shift or
> change due to circumstances, emotion, or long term vs short term**"*

**The lecturer is explicitly inviting you to look there.** My earlier advice was too strict
and would have ruled out a route the module's own guidance recommends.

---

## How to reconcile the two

They are not actually contradictory, but the join has to be made carefully.

| | Elster's claim | The lecture's claim |
|---|---|---|
| Is a preference *itself* irrational? | No | No — *"rarely irrational"* |
| Is acting on a shifted preference irrational? | No — the agent *"acts rationally"* | Not stated |
| Is a preference shift worth **investigating**? | Not discussed | **Yes — look there** |

The reconciliation: **a preference shift is a symptom, not the violation.** Elster is right
that preferring A today and B tomorrow is not, by itself, irrational. But a shift is a
**signpost** — it usually means something else in the model broke, and the lecture is
pointing you at a productive place to dig.

### The defensible version — locate the violation structurally

Look at Elster's Figure 11.1. **No arrow points into Desires.** Preferences are an *input*
to the model, never an output of it. So:

| ✅ Defensible | ❌ Overstated |
|---|---|
| "My preference shifted **because of** what I came to believe — and the model has no arrow from Beliefs or Evidence into Preferences" | "Preferences cannot change" *(Elster explicitly denies this)* |
| "The short-term preference displaced the long-term one at the moment of choice, so the action no longer served the ranking I held when I planned" | "I went against my preferences" *(L2 slide 5: not a compelling argument)* |
| "What shifted was not the preference but my **expectation** of how much I'd enjoy it" | "My preference was wrong" *(L1 slide 25: preferences cannot be intrinsically irrational)* |

**Exemplar 4 makes exactly the first move**, and it is why it reads as the strongest
analytically:

> *"The evidence of greater enjoyment when drinking then impacted my supposed immutable
> preferences going against what the model… dictates: preferences should not be affected by
> evidence or beliefs."*

Note it **also** overstates — it says preferences *"cannot be changed"*, which the set
reading contradicts. Take the structural point; drop the absolute claim.

---

## The three routes, ranked

If your decision involves a preference shift, here is how to write it up, strongest first.

### ① The shift was caused by beliefs or evidence — an illegal arrow
> *"My preference for ___ did not precede the decision; it formed once I believed ___. In
> Elster's model preferences are an input — nothing in the diagram licenses evidence
> reshaping what I want."*

Hits **marking criterion 2** (interactions between components) directly. Best option.

### ② Long-term vs short-term — the ranking reversed with no new information
> *"A week earlier I ranked ___ above ___. Nothing changed in the interval except proximity.
> The action I took did not serve the ranking I held when I formed the plan."*

Clean, self-evidencing, and the lecturer names it on slide 16.

### ③ The shift wasn't in preferences at all — it was in expectations
> *"What changed was not what I wanted but what I **expected** ___ to deliver — and that
> expectation came from my wanting it, not from evidence."*

Often the most *accurate* diagnosis. Relocates the deviation to Expectations, where the
blocked arrow lives.

---

## What is still ruled out

These remain bad answers, on both the lecture's and Elster's account:

| ❌ | Why |
|---|---|
| "I went against my preferences" | L2 slide 5 — *"not a compelling argument."* You hold conflicting preferences; every choice goes against one. |
| "My preference was irrational" | L1 slide 25 — *"preferences cannot be intrinsically irrational."* |
| "I was selfish" | Elster (2007, p. 193) — *"the confusion of rationality and egoism is a crude error."* |
| "I really wanted X but gave in to Y" | L2 slide 5 — *"avoid privileging some preferences over others."* Smuggles in a ranking the model doesn't give you. |

---

## The rule of thumb

> **A preference shift is where to look. It is not, by itself, what you report.**
> Find what caused the shift, and report *that*.
