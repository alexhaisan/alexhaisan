## The question

Economists distrust subjective survey questions. Is the distrust justified?

> *"Yet despite easy availability, this is one data source that economists rarely use. In
> fact, the unwillingness to rely on such questions marks an important divide between
> economists and other social scientists."* — p. 67, col. 1, ¶1
>
> *"These doubts are, however, based on a priori skepticism rather than on evidence… Our
> primary objective in this paper is to summarize this literature for an audience of
> economists, thereby turning a vague implicit distrust into an explicit position grounded in
> facts."* — p. 67, col. 1, ¶2

## Quote ledger — the evidence, with locators

### §I.A Cognitive Problems

| # | Finding | Locator |
|---|---|---|
| BM1 | **Question ordering.** Subjects asked "How happy are you with life in general?" and "How often do you normally go out on a date?" — *"When the dating question came first, the answers to both were highly correlated, but when it came second, they were basically uncorrelated."* | p. 67, col. 2, ¶3 |
| BM2 | **Question wording.** *"'Do you think the United States should forbid public speeches against democracy?' and 'Do you think that the United States should allow public speeches against democracy?' While more than half of the respondents stated that yes, public speeches should be 'forbidden,' three-quarters answered that no, public speeches should not be 'allowed.'"* | pp. 67–68, spanning col. 2 → col. 1 |
| BM3 | **Scale effects.** German respondents asked how many hours of TV they watch. *"Only 16 percent of the respondents given the first set of response alternatives reported watching more than 2.5 hours of TV per day, but 32 percent of the respondents given the second set of response alternatives reported watching more than 2.5 hours."* | p. 68, col. 1, ¶2 |
| BM4 | **Order of response alternatives.** GSS list of 13 qualities for a child, shown in order or reverse order: *"subjects would rate the first or last listed qualities, whatever they were, as most important."* | p. 68, col. 1, ¶4 |

### §I.B Social Desirability

| # | Finding | Locator |
|---|---|---|
| BM5 | *"roughly 25 percent of nonvoters report having voted immediately after an election. This overreporting is strongest among those who value norms of political participation the most and those who originally intended to vote."* | p. 68, col. 2, ¶1 |
| BM6 | *"reported prejudice increases when respondents believe they are being psychologically monitored for truth-telling and decreases when the survey is administered by a black person."* | p. 68, col. 2, ¶2 |

### §I.C Non-Attitudes, Wrong Attitudes, and Soft Attitudes

| # | Finding | Locator |
|---|---|---|
| BM7 | *"in two surveys spaced a few months apart, the same subjects were asked about their views on government spending. Amazingly, 55 percent of the subjects reported different answers."* | p. 68, col. 2, ¶4 |
| BM8 | *"large minorities would respond to questions about obscure or even fictitious issues, such as providing opinions on countries that do not exist."* | p. 68, col. 2, ¶5 |
| BM9 | **Two-ropes experiment.** The experimenter "accidentally" bumps a rope, setting it swinging; many more subjects then solve it. *"Yet when they are debriefed and asked how they solved the problem, few of the subjects recognize that it was the jostling by the experimenter that led them to the solution."* | pp. 68–69 |
| BM10 | **Cognitive dissonance.** Tedious task, paid a lot or very little. *"those who are paid very little report* greater *enjoyment… 'If I did not enjoy the task, why would I have done it for nothing?' … In this case, behavior shapes attitudes, and not the other way around."* | p. 69, col. 1, ¶3 |

> **BM9 and BM10 are the ones that bite hardest for your coursework.** Both say people cannot
> accurately report *why* they did what they did. That is a direct problem for an assessment
> which asks you to reconstruct your own decision process — and saying so, in one clause,
> is a genuinely sophisticated piece of self-aware evaluation.

## The conclusion — four messages (p. 71, col. 2, §IV)

> 1. *"a large experimental literature by and large supports economists' skepticism of
>    subjective questions."*
> 2. *"these findings cast serious doubts on attempts to use subjective data as **dependent
>    variables**, because the measurement error appears to correlate with a large set of
>    characteristics and behaviors."*
> 3. *"on a brighter note, these data may be useful as **explanatory variables**. One must,
>    however, take care in interpreting the results since the findings may not be causal."*
> 4. *"subjective variables are useful in practice for explaining **differences** in behavior
>    across individuals. **Changes** in answers to these questions, however, do not appear
>    useful in explaining **changes** in behavior."*

So the verdict is **qualified**, not a dismissal: usable on the right-hand side of a
regression, dangerous on the left.

## Why it matters here

- It is the **evidence behind L2 slide 15's four cons** — hypothetical bias, framing and
  order effects, social desirability, not incentivised. Cite this rather than the slide.
- **BM1–BM4 are framing effects in the wild**, which links the methods material back to the
  IRC model: a survey answer that flips on wording is an expectation shaped by something
  other than evidence.
- **BM9/BM10 turn the critique on the coursework itself.** Your 300-word piece is an act of
  introspective self-report. Acknowledging — briefly — that self-reports of reasoning are
  unreliable is the kind of move that distinguishes a strong essay in December.
