# Xiao, E. and Houser, D. (2005) 'Emotion expression in human punishment behavior'

## Reference (Harvard)

> Xiao, E. and Houser, D. (2005) 'Emotion expression in human punishment behavior', *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 102(20), pp. 7398–7401. doi: 10.1073/pnas.0502399102.

George Mason University, Interdisciplinary Center for Economic Science. Communicated by
**Vernon L. Smith**, 24 March 2005.

---

## The question

Standard ultimatum game protocols **prevent** responders from communicating anything except
by rejecting. If rejection is partly a way of **expressing anger**, then giving people a
cheaper channel for that anger should reduce rejections.

> *"Our hypothesis is that responders are less likely to choose costly punishment and
> correspondingly more likely to accept unfair outcomes when their feelings about unfair
> offers can be conveyed to proposers in an alternative, less expensive way."* — p. 7398, col. 2, ¶1

---

## Design (p. 7398, col. 2 – p. 7399, col. 1)

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Subjects | **296 undergraduates**, George Mason University, **16 sessions** |
| Treatments | **NEE** (no emotion expression) = standard UG, 62 subjects. **EE** (emotion expression) = 86 subjects |
| Stake | **$20**, divided in cents |
| EE condition | Responder may **write a message** to the proposer at no cost, delivered **concurrently** with the accept/reject decision |
| Controls | Proposers decide **before** seeing messages; all choices **anonymous**; **one-shot** |
| Payment | $5 show-up fee; average total ≈ **$12**; ~45 min in the lab |

> The concurrency matters: because proposers have already decided and the game is one-shot,
> *"messages cannot have any strategic implications"* (p. 7398). A message can only express
> feeling — which is precisely what's being tested.

### Message classification
10 independent, **hypothesis-blind** evaluators classified each of the 75 messages as
positive, negative or neutral. *"There was a single most popular classification in 71 of 75
cases"* (p. 7399, col. 1).

---

## Results

| Offer | NEE rejection | EE rejection | Locator |
|---|---|---|---|
| 60/40 | ~10% | ~10% | p. 7400, col. 1, ¶1; Fig. 1 |
| **80/20 ($4)** | **60%** (12 of 20) | **32%** (6 of 19) | p. 7399, col. 2, ¶4 |
| 80/20 exactly, responder offered 20% | **50%** (7 of 14) | **20%** (3 of 15) | p. 7399, col. 2, ¶4 |
| 90/10 | ~80% | ~75% | Fig. 1 |

Statistics: Mann–Whitney, **z = 1.757, one-tailed P = 0.04** for the 20%-or-less comparison;
**z = 1.669, one-tailed P = 0.05** for the exact 20% cell (p. 7399, col. 2).

> **Proposers' offers did not differ between treatments** (Kolmogorov–Smirnov, P = 0.80,
> p. 7399, col. 2) — so the effect is entirely on the responder side.

### The emotional content
- **~87%** of all responders wrote a message (p. 7399, col. 2)
- Of 19 responders offered 20% or less, **15 (79%) wrote a negative-emotion message, none
  positive** (p. 7399, col. 2)
- Of 36 offered at least half, **29 (81%) expressed positive emotions, none negative**

**Table 2 (p. 7400) reproduces the messages verbatim** — a genuinely striking table:
> *"Thanks For Nothing."* (accepted) · *"You suck, you are lucky I'm broke!"* (accepted) ·
> *"I don't think so buddy!"* (rejected) · *"Should not have been greedy. Oh well, you make
> nothing."* (rejected)

Note the pattern: several **accepted** the offer while writing an angry message. The money
was taken; the anger was expressed separately.

---

## The conclusion

> *"costly punishment is used by responders to express emotions and that responders are less
> likely to use costly punishment and instead accept unfair outcomes when they have a less
> expensive alternative mechanism to express negative emotions toward the proposers."*
> — p. 7400, col. 1, ¶3

---

## 🔑 Why it matters for IRC

The ultimatum game is already a deviation — IRC says accept any positive offer. Xiao and
Houser explain **why** the deviation occurs, and the explanation is not about preferences
over money at all:

| | |
|---|---|
| **IRC's account of rejection** | A preference for fairness, or for equal payoffs (Fehr and Schmidt, 1999) |
| **Xiao and Houser's account** | Rejection is an **act of expression**. Give people a cheaper way to express the same thing and rejections halve. |

That is a challenge to **instrumentality itself**, as stated on L3 slide 6: *"Instrumental
implies subjects are only concerned with outcomes — how those outcomes came to be is
irrelevant."* Here the **act** carries value independently of the outcome it produces. The
same material outcome (accepting $4) becomes acceptable once a non-material need is met
elsewhere.

> Their own reference list cites **Elster, J. (1999)** *Alchemies of the Mind: Rationality
> and the Emotions* (ref. 13) — the same Elster whose model the module is built on.
