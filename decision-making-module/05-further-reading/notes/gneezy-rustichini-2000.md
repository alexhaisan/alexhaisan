## The finding

A fine for late pickup at day-care centres **increased** late pickups, and removing the fine
did **not** reverse it.

### Abstract, verbatim (p. 1)

> *"The deterrence hypothesis predicts that the introduction of a penalty that leaves
> everything else unchanged will reduce the occurrence of the behavior subject to the fine.
> We present the result of a field study in a group of day-care centers that contradicts this
> prediction. Parents used to arrive late to collect their children, forcing a teacher to
> stay after closing time. We introduced a monetary fine for late-coming parents. As a
> result, the number of late-coming parents increased significantly. After the fine was
> removed no reduction occurred. We argue that penalties are usually introduced into an
> incomplete contract, social or private. They may change the information that agents have,
> and therefore the effect on behavior may be opposite of that expected. If this is true, the
> deterrence hypothesis loses its predictive strength, since the clause 'everything else is
> left unchanged' might be hard to satisfy."*

## The design — exact details (§II, pp. 3–5)

| Element | Detail | Locator |
|---|---|---|
| Setting | **10 private day-care centres**, Haifa, Israel | p. 3, §II.A |
| Period | January–June 1998, **20 weeks** | pp. 3–4 |
| Groups | **6 test** centres (fine) + **4 control** centres | p. 3, ¶5 |
| Weeks 1–4 | Observation only, no fine | p. 3, ¶5 |
| **Week 5** | **Fine introduced** in the 6 test centres | p. 3, ¶5 |
| **Week 17** | **Fine removed**, no explanation given | p. 5, ¶1 |
| The fine | **NIS 10 per child**, for a delay of **10 minutes or more** | p. 5, ¶1 |
| Paid to | The **owner/principal** — *not* the teacher who stayed late | p. 5, ¶1 |
| Opening hours | 0730–1600; *"Parents rarely came after 1630"* | p. 4, ¶2 |
| Monthly fee | NIS 1,400 per child (≈ US$380; US$1 ≈ NIS 3.68) | p. 4, ¶1 |
| Announcement | Note posted on the centre's bulletin board | p. 4, ¶4 |

### Was the fine big enough to matter? (§II.C, p. 5)
Their own comparisons: illegal parking **NIS 75**; running a red light **NIS 1,000 plus
penalties**; failing to collect dog droppings **NIS 360**. A babysitter earns **NIS 15–20
per hour**; average gross monthly salary in Israel at the time **NIS 5,595**.

> *"A fine of NIS 10 is relatively small but not insignificant."* — p. 5, §II.C, ¶1

**This pre-empts the obvious objection** (that the fine was simply too small to deter). Worth
citing if you use the study, because a marker will think of it.

## The results — the three Facts (§III, p. 8)

Stated verbatim in the paper as numbered facts:

> **Fact 1.** *"The effect of introducing the fine was a significant increase in the number
> of late-coming parents."*
>
> **Fact 2.** *"Removing the fine did not affect the number of late-coming parents relative
> to the time of the fine. In particular, this number remained higher in the treatment group
> than in the control group."*
>
> **Fact 3.** *"There is no significant difference in the behavior of the test group and the
> control group in the initial 4 weeks, and there is no significant trend in the test group."*

### Magnitude
> *"In the test group the number of occurrences of delay increased steadily in the first 3–4
> weeks after the introduction of the fine. The rate finally settled, at a level that was
> higher, and **almost twice as large as the initial one**."* — p. 7, ¶3

Data: Table 1 (p. 6, raw weekly counts by centre), Table 2 (p. 8, averages across four
periods), Figures 1–3 (pp. 7, 9).

## The explanation — incomplete contracts (p. 3, ¶8)

> *"What this field study teaches us, we believe, is that the introduction of the fine
> changes the perception of people regarding the environment in which they operate. In
> particular, we argue that the environment in our study, as in many real-life situations, is
> defined by an **incomplete contract**. In the specific situation under examination, the
> exact consequence of coming late was not specified in the contract between the parents and
> the day-care center… Parents could form any belief on the matter, as they probably did, and
> act accordingly. The introduction of the fine into this incomplete contract, however,
> reshapes the parents' perception of this environment."*

> **Note the mechanism carefully.** The usual gloss — "the fine crowded out a moral norm" —
> is close but not what the authors say. Their claim is about **information**: the fine told
> parents what lateness costs, and the answer turned out to be cheap. Before the fine,
> parents didn't know the price, so they behaved cautiously.
>
> Getting this right is a mark of having read it. The abstract says *"they may change the
> **information** that agents have."*

## How this maps onto Elster's IRC model

Useful because it connects Week 2's methods to Week 1's model:

| Component | What the fine changed |
|---|---|
| **Action** | Unchanged — arrive on time or late |
| **Preferences** | Unchanged — parents still prefer convenience and still care about the teacher |
| **Evidence** | **Changed** — the fine is new information about the consequence of lateness |
| **Expectations** | **Changed** — the belief "lateness is a serious imposition" is replaced by "lateness costs NIS 10" |

So the deviation isn't irrationality at all on the parents' side — it is the *experimenter's*
model that failed. **Fact 2 is the part IRC cannot absorb**: once the fine is removed, the
information is withdrawn, yet behaviour does not revert. Beliefs did not update back.
