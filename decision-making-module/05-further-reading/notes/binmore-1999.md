> ⚠️ Note the **F-prefixed page numbers** — F16–F24, not 16–24. They are part of the
> citation. Getting this right signals you had the actual article.

## The argument in one line

Economists should run their own experiments, but should only expect economic theory to
predict in the laboratory when the experimental conditions are ones the theory was ever
meant to apply to — and much of the Kahneman–Tversky programme does not meet that bar.

**The posture matters:** this is a **defence of economic theory against experimental
debunking**, written by an economist. It is not a critique of experiments as such. It is the
best source on the reading list for showing you can evaluate the evidence base rather than
just recite it.

## Binmore's three criteria (p. F17, ¶2 — bulleted in the original)

Economic theory should only be expected to predict in the laboratory if:

> - *"The problem the subjects face is not only 'reasonably' simple in itself, but is framed
>   so it seems simple to the subjects;"*
> - *"The incentives provided are 'adequate';"*
> - *"The time allowed for trial-and-error adjustment is 'sufficient'."*

**And he immediately concedes the problem with them** (B7, p. F18): the three words
*reasonably*, *adequate* and *sufficient* can be read strictly or loosely, and the reading
decides the result. He admits (p. F18, ¶1) that his *"own experience in piloting experiments
has not endowed me with any great confidence in my own ability to guess where the boundary
lies in advance."*

> This self-undermining is the most interesting thing in the paper and almost nobody will
> quote it. Using **B7** alongside the three criteria shows you read past the headline.

## Debunking vs consolidating

Binmore's organising contrast (p. F18, ¶2 onwards):

| **Debunking** motive | **Consolidating** motive |
|---|---|
| Show economic theory fails | Map where it works and where it doesn't |
| Treats a failure as a refutation | Treats a failure as a boundary to be located |

> *"Scientific experimental economists can then settle down to the twofold role of
> **consolidating** economic theory in those contexts where it works fairly well, and
> providing a source of inspiration for revising the theory where it does not work so well.
> In situations where the theory does not work at all, we need to accept our limitations and
> look to disciplines like psychology for the answers."* — p. F17, ¶7

That last clause matters: Binmore is **not** claiming economics explains everything.

## The two case studies

### Case Study 1 — Two-person zero-sum games (pp. F18–F19)
Von Neumann's (1928) minimax theory went unconfirmed in experiments for decades. Binmore's
diagnosis: the experiments were badly built. Estes (1957) *"subjects were not even told that
they were playing against an opponent"* (p. F18). When Binmore et al. (1996) gave subjects
real-time graphical feedback comparing their payoff with the median subject's, *"the minimax
theory proved to be well supported"* (p. F19, ¶2).

Anticipated objection and reply (p. F19, ¶3): critics say rich feedback just *teaches*
subjects the answer. Binmore: *"being provided with information about how successful you are
compared with other people in the same position as yourself is not the same as being told
what to do by an authority figure."*

### Case Study 2 — The Ultimatum Game (pp. F19–F23)
Standard result: proposers offer near 50:50; responders reject below about a third. Binmore
notes both the fairness-norm explanation and the exotic-preferences explanation fit the data,
and that **neither explains it** (B9, p. F20). His own account (p. F22): fairness norms
evolved as *"an efficient solution to the equilibrium selection problem"* in repeated games,
and behaviour **changes over time** as subjects learn the norm doesn't fit the situation.

> Directly relevant if you use the ultimatum game in December — Binmore is the counterweight
> to the standard behavioural reading of it.

## How to use it

| Purpose | Use |
|---|---|
| Critically evaluate experimental evidence | B5, B6, B10 — the dirty test tubes line |
| Show the criteria are contestable | B7 + B8 |
| Distinguish describing from explaining | B9 |
| Challenge a behavioural finding you cite | B2, B3 |
| Frame the whole method section | The debunking/consolidating contrast |

**Caution:** Binmore is partisan and writes like it. Present him as *one side of a live
methodological argument*, with Croson and Gächter (2010) as the more even-handed statement.
Treating him as the settled view would be a misreading.
