# Kosfeld, M. et al. (2005) 'Oxytocin increases trust in humans'

**Status:** ✅ VERIFIED — read in full, 22 Oct 2026 · Week 3–4 reading

## Reference (Harvard)

> Kosfeld, M., Heinrichs, M., Zak, P.J., Fischbacher, U. and Fehr, E. (2005) 'Oxytocin increases trust in humans', *Nature*, 435(7042), pp. 673–676. doi: 10.1038/nature03701.

Published 2 June 2005. Affiliations: Universities of Zurich; Claremont Graduate University.
Received 20 April, accepted 5 May 2005.

---

## Design (Methods, pp. 675–676)

| Element | Detail |
|---|---|
| Subjects | **194 healthy male students** from Zurich universities, mean age **22.0 ± 3.4** |
| Split | Trust experiment **128**; risk experiment **66** |
| Substance | **24 IU intranasal oxytocin** (Syntocinon-Spray, Novartis; 3 puffs per nostril, 4 IU each) or placebo, **50 min before** the game |
| Design | **Double-blind, placebo-controlled**, randomised |
| Game | Trust game. Both get **12 MU**. Investor sends **0, 4, 8 or 12**; experimenter **triples** it; trustee returns **0 to 48** |
| Decisions | Four per subject, same role, randomly re-paired, **no feedback between decisions** |
| Payment | Flat 80 Swiss francs + earnings at 0.40 CHF per MU |

### The crucial control: the **risk experiment**
Identical, except a **random mechanism** replaces the trustee's decision. Same risk, same
payoffs — but **no social interaction**. This isolates whether oxytocin affects trust
specifically or just risk-taking generally.

## Results

| Measure | Oxytocin | Placebo | Locator |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mean average transfer, **trust** experiment | **9.6 MU** | **8.1 MU** | Table 1, p. 674 |
| Median average transfer, trust | **10 MU** | **8 MU** | Table 1 |
| Showed **maximal** trust (12 MU) | **45%** (13/29) | **21%** (6/29) | p. 674, col. 1, ¶3 |
| Trust level **below 8 MU** | 21% | 45% | p. 674, col. 1, ¶3 |
| Mean average transfer, **risk** experiment | **7.5 MU** | **7.5 MU** | Table 1 |

> *"The investors' average transfer is **17% higher** in the oxytocin group (Mann–Whitney
> U-test; z = −1.897, P = 0.029, one-sided)."* — p. 674, col. 1, ¶3
>
> *"oxytocin increases the investors' transfer levels in the trust experiment but **not in
> the risk experiment**."* — p. 674, col. 2, ¶3

### Three findings that matter more than the headline

**① It is not general risk-seeking.** The risk experiment shows no effect at all — identical
medians (8 MU) and means (7.5 MU) in both groups. The abstract: *"the effect of oxytocin on
trust is **not** due to a general increase in the readiness to bear risks. On the contrary,
oxytocin specifically affects an individual's willingness to accept **social** risks arising
through interpersonal interactions"* (p. 673).

**② Trustees given oxytocin were no more trustworthy.** Fig. 3, p. 675: back transfers are
*"statistically indistinguishable"* between oxytocin and placebo trustees at every positive
transfer level. *"oxytocin does not increase the general inclination to behave prosocially.
Rather, oxytocin specifically affects the trusting behaviour of investors"* (p. 675, col. 1).

**③ 🔑 It did not change beliefs.** The authors measured investors' expected back transfers:
> *"these expectations do not differ significantly between oxytocin and placebo subjects at
> any feasible positive transfer level… **the investors given oxytocin show more trusting
> behaviour but do not hold significantly different beliefs about the trustworthiness of
> others**."* — p. 675, col. 1, ¶3

Mood and calmness were also unchanged (p. 674).

## 🔑 Why this is a sharp problem for IRC

Elster's model says the **action** follows from **preferences** + **expectations**. Kosfeld
et al. changed the action **while leaving expectations constant** — same beliefs about
trustworthiness, same assessment of risk, same mood, different behaviour.

| Component | Changed by oxytocin? |
|---|---|
| Action | **Yes** — transfers 17% higher |
| Expectations/Beliefs | **No** — measured, no significant difference |
| Evidence | **No** — identical information |
| Preferences | Unclear — arguably what shifted, but not via any route the model permits |

So something is driving Action that enters through **none of the model's four boxes and none
of its arrows**. That is a structural challenge, not merely an anomaly — and it hits
**marking criterion 2** (interactions between components) squarely.

The authors' own interpretation is **betrayal aversion** (p. 675): oxytocin helps subjects
overcome the specific aversion to being betrayed by a person, as distinct from bearing risk.

## Caveats worth stating if you use it

- **All-male sample** (n = 194), all university students, all Zurich — external validity
- Single dose, single setting
- Intranasal oxytocin dosing has since been debated in the literature; replication of social
  effects has been contested. ⚠️ **I have not verified the replication record** — check
  before making strong claims
- Binmore (1999) would ask whether the lab conditions were ones theory should apply to

> The authors themselves flag the dual-use point (p. 675): *"this knowledge could be misused
> to induce trusting behaviours that selfish actors subsequently exploit."*
