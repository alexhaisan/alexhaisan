## ✅ READ — Elster (2007), ch. 11 'Rational Choice', pp. 191–213
### *SET READING*

Full notes: `notes/elster-2007-ch11.md`

**① The crossed arrow in Figure 11.1.** Of everything on this list, this is the detail I'd
most want in your coursework. Desires may *not* flow directly into beliefs — that's wishful
thinking — but they *may* flow into beliefs **via** evidence-gathering. Elster's phrase is
perfect: the wish must not be *"the father of the thought"*, but desires can be *"the
**grandfather** of the beliefs."* It gives you a razor-sharp way to say what went wrong if
your decision involved any element of believing what you wanted to believe.

**② "It is not enough to do the right thing by fluke."** The arrows carry **causality** as
well as optimality. This quietly rules out a whole class of weak answers, and it gives you
the smokers example: two opposite biases can cancel and produce the *correct* belief, and
the person is still not rational. Rationality is about the **process**, all the way down.

**What I like about it:** it's a philosopher writing for people who aren't philosophers.
The model is given in two pages and a diagram, then he spends twenty pages telling you where
it breaks — including the admission that it often can't predict anything at all
(**indeterminacy**). That intellectual honesty is rare in a set reading, and it's where your
evaluation marks live.

## ✅ READ — Hargreaves Heap (1992), ch. 1 'Rationality', pp. 3–25
### *SET READING*

Full notes: `notes/hargreaves-heap-1992-ch1.md`

**① It answers the lecturer's actual question.** *"What's the difference between 'Rational
Choice' and 'Instrumental Rational Choice'?"* — this chapter is the answer. Three senses:
**instrumental** (means to given ends), **procedural** (rules and norms), **expressive**
(deliberating on the ends themselves). Instrumental is the narrow one where *"reason can
assess only their consistency, not their content."*

**② The information regress.** This is the cleverest argument in the whole week. IRC says
gather information until marginal benefit equals marginal cost. But how do you know the
marginal benefit *without already having the information*? *"From a point just below the
summit of the foothills, there may seem little advantage in climbing to the top. Yet an
aerial photograph would reveal that the tops of the mountains which lie beyond afford
unimaginably better views."* It attacks Elster's third optimality requirement **from inside
the model**, not from psychology — which makes it much harder to dismiss.

**What I like about it:** the honesty of the conclusion. He doesn't declare a winner —
*"they should not be regarded as competitors: rather, they provide complementary insights."*
And the box on **multiple selves** (Bismarck: *"Faust complained that he had two souls in his
breast. I have a whole squabbling crowd"*) is the most human moment in either reading.

## 📖 Plous, ch. 7 — *The Psychology of Judgment and Decision Making* (1993)

⚠️ **I'm not certain what chapter 7 covers.** The book is organised into parts (perception
and memory; how questions affect answers; models of decision making; heuristics and biases;
the social side), and chapter 7 sits around the boundary between question-framing and formal
decision models. Given it's set for the IRC week, it is most likely the **expected
utility / normative models** chapter — but **check when you open it.**

**What the book is for:** it is the most *readable* introduction to this field that exists,
and it is written by a psychologist for people who are not economists. Plous reproduces the
original experimental materials — you read the actual Asian Disease wording, the actual Linda
problem — so the experiments land rather than being summarised at you.

**What I'd take from it:** Plous is the right source if you want to *describe an experiment
accurately and briefly*. For the December essay, where you need an experiment in a few
sentences, his write-ups are the model to imitate.

## 📖 Wilkinson, ch. 1 — *An Introduction to Behavioral Economics*

Chapter 1 is 'The Nature of Behavioral Economics' — the standard model and its assumptions,
the history (Smith and Bentham were far more psychological than the neoclassicals who
followed), and the methodological objections to behavioural economics.

⚠️ Editions differ: Wilkinson (2008, 1st ed.), Wilkinson & Klaes (2012, 2nd; 2018, 3rd).
Check which the library has, and cite that one.

**① It gives you the historical arc**, which neither set reading does: economics *started*
psychological, deliberately purged the psychology in the late 19th/early 20th century to
become mathematically tractable, and has spent forty years putting it back. That's a strong
opening framing for the December essay.

**② It takes the objections to behavioural economics seriously** — that it's a collection of
anomalies rather than a theory, that lab findings don't generalise, that markets discipline
irrationality. Knowing the counter-arguments is what separates evaluation from description.

## 📖 Baron, chs. 3 & 10 — *Thinking and Deciding*

⚠️ Chapter numbering shifts between editions (4th ed. 2008 is the common one). On my
understanding **ch. 3 is 'Rationality'** and **ch. 10 the normative theory of choice under
uncertainty** (i.e. expected utility) — a sensible pairing for this week. **Verify.**

**① The normative / descriptive / prescriptive trichotomy.** This is Baron's signature
contribution and it is genuinely useful here:
- **Normative** — the standard against which we judge (IRC)
- **Descriptive** — what people actually do (the behavioural findings)
- **Prescriptive** — what we should *advise*, given that people are as they are

Your module maps onto it exactly: learning objective 1 is normative, objective 2 is
descriptive, objective 3 is prescriptive. If you want one framework to structure the
December essay, this is a strong candidate.

**② Rationality is a property of thinking, not of outcomes.** Baron is firm that rational
thinking is *"whatever kind of thinking best helps people achieve their goals"* — judged by
the process, not the result. It's the same point as Elster p. 209, from a psychologist
rather than a philosopher, and having it from two directions makes it much more citable.

## 📖 Allingham, M. (1999) *Rational Choice*. Macmillan

A short, formal treatment — preference and utility, choice under certainty, risk and
uncertainty, then outward to strategic and social choice.

**What I'd take from it:** use it as a **reference**, not a read-through. If you need the
precise statement of an axiom or a clean definition for the definitional opening of your
coursework, this is where to get it right. It's the most compact formal source on the list.

> Note: Allingham also wrote *Choice Theory: A Very Short Introduction* (OUP, 2002), which is
> easier to find and covers much of the same ground. Don't confuse the two when citing.

## 📖 Elster, J. (1989) *Nuts and Bolts for the Social Sciences*, ch. 3

The **earlier, shorter** statement of the same model you've already read. The 2007 book is
explicitly the sequel (*"More Nuts and Bolts"*).

⚠️ The reading list says ch. 3. Depending on edition, ch. 3 may be **'Desires and
Opportunities'** with rational choice following in ch. 4. **Check which you've got.**

**① If it is 'Desires and Opportunities':** the argument is that **opportunities often do
more explanatory work than desires** — constraints are observable, desires are not, so when
explaining behaviour you should look at what was *possible* before you speculate about what
was *wanted*. Applied to your coursework, that's a prompt to be rigorous about the real
feasible set before you start theorising about your motives.

**② Either way, this is the source the lecture slides actually cite** ("Elster 1989",
slides 14 and 16). If you quote the slide's definition of rational choice — *"when faced with
several courses of action, people usually do what they believe is likely to have the best
overall outcome"* — this is the book it comes from, and citing it correctly is a cheap mark.

## 📖 Hogarth, R. and Reder, M. (1987) *Rational Choice: The Contrast between Economics and Psychology*

A conference volume from the 1985 University of Chicago meeting where economists and
psychologists were put in a room together. Contributors include **Tversky & Kahneman**
('Rational Choice and the Framing of Decisions'), **Arrow**, **Simon**, **Thaler** and
**Lucas**.

**① It is the primary document of the disciplinary clash**, not a retrospective account of
it. You can watch Lucas argue that irrationality gets competed away and Tversky argue that it
doesn't, in their own words, at the moment it mattered.

**② Tversky & Kahneman's chapter in it is a genuinely important paper** in its own right —
the clearest statement of why framing effects are a problem for *normative* theory and not
just a curiosity. If you end up writing about framing in December, cite that chapter rather
than a textbook summary of it.

## 📖 Sugden, R. (1991) 'Rational Choice: A Survey of Contributions from Economics and Philosophy', *Economic Journal*, 101, pp. 751–785

A survey of where economics and philosophy meet on rationality. Sugden is also one of the
co-authors of the Hargreaves Heap volume you've been set, and co-developer (with Loomes) of
**regret theory**.

**① It is the right source for the "is consistency *enough*?" question.** IRC says reason
assesses only the consistency of preferences. Sugden surveys the arguments for and against
that being the whole story. That's precisely the question the lecturer is pointing at with
*"what's the difference between Rational Choice and Instrumental Rational Choice?"*

**② Regret theory is worth knowing about** as an alternative that keeps the formal
machinery but drops a single axiom — a useful corrective to the impression that the only
options are "IRC" or "psychology".

## 📖 Schoemaker, P.J.H. (1982) 'The Expected Utility Model: Its Variants, Purposes, Evidence and Limitations', *Journal of Economic Literature*, 20(2), pp. 529–563

**① The single most useful idea on this whole list, for your purposes:** Schoemaker separates
the **purposes** expected utility can be put to — *descriptive, predictive, postdictive,
prescriptive, normative* — and argues much of the confusion in the literature comes from
treating a failure at one as a failure at all of them.

This matters directly for your coursework. When you say your decision "deviated from IRC",
you are saying the model failed **descriptively**. You are *not* saying it failed
**normatively** — in fact your argument depends on it still being the right standard, or
there'd be nothing to deviate *from*. Making that distinction explicitly, in one clause, is
the kind of thing that reads as genuine understanding rather than recitation.

**② It's a survey**, so it's also the efficient route to the experimental violations
(Allais, Ellsberg, preference reversals) with citations attached.

## 📖 Gneezy, U. and Rustichini, A. (2000) 'A Fine Is a Price', *Journal of Legal Studies*, 29(1), pp. 1–17

Israeli day-care centres introduced a small fine for collecting children late. **Late
collections increased** — and stayed high after the fine was withdrawn.

**① The result is a direct, clean contradiction of a first-order IRC prediction.** Raise the
price of a behaviour, get less of it. They got more. And the *irreversibility* is the
sharpest part: the fine converted a **moral obligation** into a **purchasable commodity**,
and removing the fine did not restore the norm.

**② It connects to Hargreaves-Heap's procedural rationality** rather than to any bias. The
parents weren't making a cognitive error — they were operating under a different *rule*. That
makes it a much more interesting citation than yet another bias, and it's a bridge between
the two set readings.

## 🎥 The Ayn Rand interview

The lecturer's framing: *"she was one of the strongest proponents of the importance of
rational decision-making... and (probably unintentionally) gives some of its flaws by
implication — if you find yourself thinking 'but that's not how real people make decisions!',
you're seeing some of the problems with IRC."* First ~6 minutes are the relevant part.

**① Rand is a live demonstration of the error Elster names on p. 193.** He writes: *"There is
no implication that the desires be selfish. **The confusion of rationality and egoism is a
crude error**."* Rand's whole philosophy is that confusion, worked out systematically and
defended with total conviction. Watching it makes the distinction stick far better than
reading the sentence does.

**② And — the sharper point — Rand isn't actually *instrumentally* rational at all.** She
holds that reason can determine what you *ought to value*, which is exactly what instrumental
rationality denies ("reason is slave to the passions"). In Hargreaves-Heap's terms, Rand is
closer to **expressive** rationality: reason deliberating on ends. So she manages to be both
*too thick* (smuggling egoism into the definition) and *not instrumental* (letting reason
choose the ends).

> That observation — that the module's loudest advocate of "rationality" is advocating
> something the module's own model excludes — is a genuinely good evaluative point, and
> almost nobody else will make it.
