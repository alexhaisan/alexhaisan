# 01 — Bounded Rationality & Satisficing (Simon)

**Attacks IRC component:** (5) the optimisation rule, and Elster condition **(iii)** —
optimal search.

---

## Core claim

Humans are **"intendedly rational, but only limitedly so"** (Simon, 1947). Rationality is
bounded by three things:

1. **Cognitive limits** — finite memory, attention and computational capacity
2. **Information limits** — incomplete and costly-to-acquire information
3. **Time limits** — decisions must be made before the analysis could ever be complete

Given those bounds, agents do not **maximise** — they **satisfice**: they search until they
find the first option that clears an **aspiration level**, then stop.

> *"Decision makers can satisfice either by finding optimum solutions for a simplified world,
> or by finding satisfactory solutions for a more realistic world."* — Simon (1979)

## Mechanism

| | Maximising (IRC) | Satisficing (Simon) |
|---|---|---|
| Options considered | All in the feasible set | Sequential, until threshold met |
| Stopping rule | When the best is identified | When "good enough" is found |
| Standard | Optimum | **Aspiration level** |
| Aspiration level | n/a | **Adjusts** up after easy success, down after repeated failure |

The adaptive aspiration level is the elegant part: it makes satisficing *responsive* to the
environment without requiring global optimisation.

## Related concepts

- **Scissors metaphor** (Simon, 1990): behaviour is cut by two blades — the structure of
  the task environment and the computational capacities of the actor. Neither alone explains it.
- **Ecological rationality** (Gigerenzer & Goldstein, 1996): heuristics are not defective
  shortcuts but **"fast and frugal"** rules that can *outperform* complex models in
  environments with the right structure. This is a **defence** of heuristics, in direct
  tension with Kahneman & Tversky's error-focused framing. Mentioning this tension signals
  genuine critical engagement — but it costs words.
- **Choice overload** (Iyengar & Lepper, 2000): the jam study — 24 varieties attracted more
  browsers but 6 varieties produced ~10× the purchase rate. More options ⇒ worse outcomes,
  which IRC cannot accommodate (a larger feasible set is weakly better by construction).
- **Rational inattention** (Sims, 2003): attention is itself a scarce resource that is
  allocated optimally — a *within*-IRC repair rather than a rejection of it.

## Using it in the coursework

**Fits if:** you chose the first adequate option; you stopped researching early; you were
overwhelmed by options and deferred or defaulted; you used a simplifying rule because the
full comparison was infeasible.

**Does not fit if:** you *did* deliberate thoroughly and still chose badly — that is a
different failure (condition (i) or (ii), not (iii)).

**Specimen sentence (≈35 words):**
> *Rather than maximising over the full feasible set, I satisficed (Simon, 1955): I searched
> sequentially and stopped at the first option clearing my aspiration level, leaving superior
> alternatives in the choice set unexamined.*

## Future-application ideas (component D)

- **Set the aspiration level before searching**, in writing, with explicit criteria — this
  converts an adaptive, drifting threshold into a fixed standard.
- **Pre-commit to a search budget** ("I will compare exactly five options, then decide"),
  which optimises condition (iii) explicitly instead of leaving it to fatigue.
- **Secretary-rule style stopping**: sample ~37% of the field to calibrate, then take the
  next option that beats everything seen. A defensible, teachable stopping rule.
- **Reduce the choice set deliberately** before deciding, rather than enlarging it.
