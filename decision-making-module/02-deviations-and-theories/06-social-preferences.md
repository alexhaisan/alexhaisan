# 06 — Social Preferences, Fairness and Identity

**Attacks IRC component:** the **thick** self-interest assumption — and, where norms make
the *act* valuable independently of consequences, component (6) consequentialism.

> ⚠️ **Handle with care in this coursework.** Under *thin* rationality — which the brief's
> phrase *"with your preferences"* commits you to — other-regarding preferences are
> perfectly admissible. "I was generous, therefore irrational" is **wrong** and markers will
> catch it.
>
> This theory family only yields a genuine deviation when the social motive produces
> **inconsistency**: a choice that varies with framing, context, or observation while the
> material consequences stay identical.

## The canonical experiments

### Ultimatum Game (Güth, Schmittberger & Schwarze, 1982)
Proposer splits £10; responder accepts (split stands) or rejects (both get nothing).

| | Prediction | Observed |
|---|---|---|
| Proposer offers | Minimum positive amount (£0.01) | Modal offer ≈ 40–50% |
| Responder accepts | Any positive amount | **Rejects offers below ~20–30% about half the time** |

Rejection is the striking result: it is **costly punishment**. The responder pays real money
to deny the proposer an unfair gain.

### Dictator Game
Remove the rejection option. Self-interest predicts zero transfer. Observed: ~20% on
average, with substantial heterogeneity. Shows the proposer's generosity is not *only*
strategic fear of rejection.

### Public Goods / Trust games
Contribution starts around 40–60% of endowment and **decays with repetition** — unless
costly punishment is available, which sustains cooperation (Fehr & Gächter, 2000).

## Formal models

**Inequity aversion** (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999): utility is reduced by *both* disadvantageous
and advantageous inequality:

$$U_i = x_i - \alpha_i \max(x_j - x_i, 0) - \beta_i \max(x_i - x_j, 0), \qquad \alpha_i \ge \beta_i \ge 0$$

Note this is a **repair** of IRC, not a rejection: it keeps maximisation and simply enriches
the utility function. Worth saying, if you have the words — it shows you understand that
"behavioural" findings vary in how deep a challenge they pose.

**Reciprocity** (Rabin, 1993): intentions matter, not just outcomes — kindness is repaid,
unkindness punished.

**Identity** (Akerlof & Kranton, 2000): utility depends on acting in accordance with the
norms of a social category one identifies with.

---

## Crowding out

**Gneezy & Rustichini (2000), "A fine is a price":** Israeli day-care centres introduced a
fine for late pickup. Late pickups **increased** — and stayed high after the fine was removed.
The fine converted a **social obligation** into a **market transaction**, destroying the norm.

> IRC predicts a price increase reduces the behaviour. The opposite happened. This is a
> strong, memorable result, and unusually easy to deploy in 300 words.

Related: **motivation crowding** (Frey & Jegen, 2001) — extrinsic incentives can displace
intrinsic motivation.

## Using it in the coursework

**Fits if:** you rejected a materially advantageous option because it felt unfair; you
behaved differently when observed than when not; an explicit incentive changed your
motivation in the wrong direction; you acted to preserve self-image or identity against
your own material interest *and* that behaviour reverses under reframing.

**Does not fit if:** you simply had altruistic preferences and acted on them consistently.
**That is rational.** Do not submit it.

**Specimen sentence (≈35 words):**
> *Material payoff maximisation predicts acceptance; I rejected. Consistent with ultimatum
> game evidence (Güth et al., 1982) and inequity aversion (Fehr & Schmidt, 1999), I incurred
> a real cost to punish a distribution I coded as unfair.*

## Future-application ideas (component D)

- **Separate the fairness judgement from the payoff decision** — decide whether to accept on
  material grounds first, then decide separately whether the relationship warrants a
  response. Two decisions, taken in order, not one fused one.
- **Ask what the same offer would look like from a stranger** — tests whether the reaction
  is about the offer or about the relationship.
- **Watch for crowding out when designing incentives** (directly relevant to the module's
  organisational/managerial economics strand): a price can destroy a norm, and the norm may
  be more valuable than the price.
