# 05 — Mental Accounting & the Sunk Cost Fallacy (Thaler)

**Attacks IRC components:** (6) consequentialism — directly; (2) preferences — via
**fungibility** failure.

---

## A. Mental accounting

### Core claim
People **code, categorise and evaluate** financial outcomes in separate mental "accounts"
(Thaler, 1985; 1999). IRC requires money to be **fungible** — £1 is £1 regardless of its
source or label. Mental accounting says it is not.

### Mechanisms

| Mechanism | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Non-fungibility** | A £200 windfall is spent differently from £200 of salary |
| **Budget categories** | Separate "entertainment", "groceries", "holiday" budgets; overspend in one is not offset against another |
| **Transaction utility** | Value = *acquisition utility* (the good itself) + *transaction utility* (the deal relative to a reference price). Buying something you don't need because "it was 70% off" is pure transaction utility |
| **Narrow bracketing** | Decisions evaluated one at a time rather than as a portfolio |
| **Payment decoupling** | Pre-paid or card payments feel cheaper than cash; the pain of paying is separated from consumption |
| **House money effect** | Winnings are gambled more freely than "own" money (Thaler & Johnson, 1990) |

### Evidence
- **Theatre ticket problem** (Kahneman & Tversky, 1984): people who lost a £10 *ticket* are
  far less willing to buy a replacement than people who lost a £10 *note* — although the
  wealth position is identical. The ticket loss is posted to the "theatre" account; the cash
  loss is not.
- **NYC cabdrivers** (Camerer et al., 1997): drivers set a *daily income target* and quit
  once it is met — so they work **fewer** hours on busy, high-wage days. The intertemporal
  substitution is exactly backwards from the labour supply model.

## B. Sunk cost fallacy

### Core claim
IRC is **consequentialist**: only *future* costs and benefits are decision-relevant. Costs
already incurred and unrecoverable are, by definition, irrelevant to any choice made now.
People nonetheless **honour sunk costs** — continuing a losing course of action *because* of
what has already been spent (Arkes & Blumer, 1985).

> **Why it is unambiguously a deviation:** this is one of the cleanest cases for the
> coursework, because there is no way to recast it as a preference. A preference over
> *future* states cannot be served by attending to an *unrecoverable past* expenditure.
> The counterfactual is airtight.

### Evidence
- **Arkes & Blumer (1985), theatre season tickets:** buyers randomly assigned a discount
  attended *fewer* performances than full-price buyers, despite identical future benefit.
- **Ski trip experiment:** participants chose the less enjoyable trip because they had paid
  more for it.
- **Escalation of commitment** (Staw, 1976): decision-makers allocate *more* resources to a
  failing project when they were personally responsible for the initial commitment —
  self-justification amplifies the effect.
- **Concorde fallacy**: the canonical policy example.

### Mechanisms behind it
1. **Loss aversion** — abandoning converts a *paper* loss into a *realised* one
2. **Self-justification / cognitive dissonance** (Festinger, 1957) — admitting waste
   threatens self-image as a competent decision-maker
3. **Waste avoidance** — a norm against appearing wasteful, which the sunk cost framing
   perversely inverts

## Using it in the coursework

**Fits if:** you continued a course of action because of money, time or effort already
invested; you finished something you were not enjoying because you had paid; you kept a
project going past the point of value; you treated a windfall differently from earned money;
you bought something because it was discounted.

**Does not fit if:** continuing was genuinely optimal on forward-looking grounds (learning
value, reputation, contractual penalty) — check this honestly first, because markers look
for it.

**Specimen sentence (≈35 words):**
> *Instrumental rationality is consequentialist: only prospective costs are decision-relevant.
> I nonetheless honoured an unrecoverable expenditure (Arkes & Blumer, 1985), because
> abandoning would have realised a loss my value function weights roughly twice as heavily.*

## Future-application ideas (component D)

- **The fresh-start test:** *"If I were arriving at this situation today, with no history and
  no money spent, would I choose to begin?"* If no, stop. This is a one-sentence,
  implementable rule and it directly operationalises consequentialism.
- **Separate the decision-maker from the original committer** — ask someone uninvolved, or
  deliberately adopt an outsider's view, to neutralise self-justification.
- **Pre-set kill criteria** at the start of any project: define in advance the observable
  conditions under which you will stop. Decided cold, enforced hot.
- **Broad bracketing** of budgets — treat money as fungible by reviewing all accounts
  together, monthly.
