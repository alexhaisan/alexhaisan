## The IRC benchmark: exponential discounting

The standard model (Samuelson, 1937) discounts future utility **exponentially**:

$$U = \sum_{t=0}^{T} \delta^{t} u(c_t), \qquad 0 < \delta < 1$$

The critical property is **time consistency**: the trade-off between two future dates
depends only on the *interval* between them, never on how far away they both are. A plan
made today is still optimal tomorrow. **No preference reversal is possible.**

## The behavioural alternative: hyperbolic / quasi-hyperbolic discounting

Observed discounting is **hyperbolic** — steep in the near term, flat in the long term
(Ainslie, 1975; Thaler, 1981). The tractable form is **quasi-hyperbolic (β–δ)**
(Laibson, 1997):

$$U = u(c_0) + \beta \sum_{t=1}^{T} \delta^{t} u(c_t), \qquad 0 < \beta < 1$$

The **β** term applies a single extra discount to *everything that is not now*. It creates a
sharp discontinuity between the present and the entire future.

### The consequence: preference reversal

> Asked in advance, most people prefer **£110 in 31 days** to **£100 in 30 days**.
> Asked on day 30, many of the same people take the **£100 today**.
>
> Nothing changed except the passage of time. No new information arrived. The *same* one-day
> delay for the *same* £10 is now judged differently. **This is a violation of IRC that
> cannot be explained away as a preference** — because it is the same person's preference
> contradicting itself.

This is why present bias is such a strong coursework choice: the preference reversal is
self-evidencing. You do not need to argue that your preference was wrong, only that it was
*inconsistent with your own earlier preference*.

## Sophistication vs naïveté (O'Donoghue & Rabin, 1999)

| Type | Belief about future self | Behaviour |
|---|---|---|
| **Naïf** | Believes β = 1 in future; "I'll do it tomorrow" | Procrastinates indefinitely; repeatedly surprised |
| **Sophisticate** | Knows β < 1 in future | Seeks **commitment devices** to constrain the future self |
| **Partial naïf** | Underestimates own future bias | Most realistic case |

This distinction is directly useful for component D: **the move from naïf to sophisticate is
exactly "what you have learned".** You now know your β < 1, so you build around it instead
of resolving to try harder.

## Related

- **Ulysses contracts / commitment devices**: binding the future self. Ulysses and the mast;
  Christmas savings clubs; gym pre-payment; Save More Tomorrow.
- **Save More Tomorrow** (Thaler & Benartzi, 2004): commit *now* to allocating *future* pay
  rises to savings. Exploits present bias rather than fighting it — savings rates rose from
  3.5% to 13.6% over 40 months.
- **Visceral factors** (Loewenstein, 1996): hunger, fatigue, arousal, craving and pain
  cause a temporary, state-dependent collapse of the discount function. The **hot–cold
  empathy gap** — in a cold state you cannot predict how you will behave in a hot state.
- **Projection bias** (Loewenstein, O'Donoghue & Rabin, 2003): you project your current
  state onto your future self (shopping while hungry).

## Using it in the coursework

**Fits if:** you made a plan and then broke it; you knew the better option and took the
worse one *because it was available now*; you procrastinated; you over-consumed in the
moment; you reversed a decision with no new information.

**Does not fit if:** there was no time dimension, or you simply changed your mind on
receiving new evidence (that is rational Bayesian updating, not inconsistency).

**Specimen sentence (≈40 words):**
> *My discount function is quasi-hyperbolic rather than exponential (Laibson, 1997): the
> present-biased parameter β<1 disproportionately weighted the immediate reward, producing a
> preference reversal relative to the plan I had made a week earlier — a dynamic inconsistency
> IRC excludes by construction.*

## Future-application ideas (component D)

> ⚠️ Avoid "I will have more willpower." Present bias predicts that will fail. The *correct*
> lesson from this literature is **architectural, not motivational**.

- **Commitment device** with a real cost of defection (pre-payment, deposit contract, a
  stake held by a third party, a public pledge).
- **Remove the option at the moment of weakness** — don't keep it in the house; delete the
  app; unlink the saved card. You are a sophisticate constraining the naïf.
- **Bright-line rules** rather than case-by-case judgement — case-by-case is exactly where
  β bites.
- **Automate the choice away** (standing order, auto-enrolment), so the future self never
  faces the decision.
- **Implementation intentions** (Gollwitzer, 1999): specify *when, where and how* — "if X,
  then I will do Y". Converts an intention into a trigger-response.
