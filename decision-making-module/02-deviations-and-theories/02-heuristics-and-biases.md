**Attacks IRC component:** (3) beliefs, and Elster condition **(ii)** — belief proportioned
to evidence.

---

## Core claim

People rely on a small number of **judgemental heuristics** to reduce complex probability
assessments to simpler operations. These heuristics are useful but produce **systematic,
predictable errors** — biases — not random noise (Tversky & Kahneman, 1974).

The systematic part is what matters. Random error is consistent with IRC (it averages out).
**Directional, predictable error is not.**

---

## The three original heuristics

### 1. Availability
Probability is judged by **how easily instances come to mind**. Ease of recall is driven by
recency, vividness, emotional salience and media coverage — none of which track frequency.

> *Classic:* people overestimate deaths from shark attacks, plane crashes and homicide;
> underestimate strokes, diabetes and asthma. The gap tracks newsworthiness, not statistics.

**Fits if:** a recent or vivid instance drove your probability estimate.

### 2. Representativeness
Probability is judged by **similarity to a stereotype or prototype**, ignoring:
- **Base rates** — the "Linda problem" and the "Tom W." engineer/lawyer studies
- **Sample size** — the "law of small numbers"; the hospital problem
- **Regression to the mean** — extreme outcomes are expected to persist
- **Conjunction** — "feminist bank teller" judged more likely than "bank teller"

> The **conjunction fallacy** is logically impossible: $p(A \cap B)$ can never exceed $p(A)$.
> It is the cleanest demonstration that judgement is not probability-theoretic.

**Fits if:** you judged by "this looks like a…" rather than by frequency.

### 3. Anchoring and adjustment
An initial value — however arbitrary — **anchors** the estimate, and subsequent adjustment
is **insufficient**.

> *Classic:* a spun wheel of fortune landing on 10 vs 65 shifted estimates of the percentage
> of African nations in the UN (25% vs 45%). The anchor was transparently random and still
> moved judgements. Ariely, Loewenstein & Prelec (2003) showed the same with social security
> digits anchoring willingness-to-pay for goods — **"coherent arbitrariness"**.

**Fits if:** a list price, an opening offer, a first quote or an arbitrary number framed
your valuation.

## Other biases worth knowing

| Bias | Description | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Overconfidence** | Confidence intervals far too narrow; "better than average" effect | Alpert & Raiffa (1982); Svenson (1981) |
| **Confirmation bias** | Seek and over-weight evidence supporting a held hypothesis | Wason (1960); Nickerson (1998) |
| **Hindsight bias** | "I knew it all along" — past events seem more predictable than they were | Fischhoff (1975) |
| **Optimism bias / planning fallacy** | Systematic underestimation of time, cost and risk for one's own projects | Kahneman & Tversky (1979b); Buehler et al. (1994) |
| **Hot hand / gambler's fallacy** | Misreading randomness as streaks or as "due" | Gilovich, Vallone & Tversky (1985) |
| **Affect heuristic** | Judging risk and benefit by how one *feels* about the thing | Slovic et al. (2002) |

## Using it in the coursework

**Fits if:** your *belief* was wrong in a patterned way, given evidence you actually had.

**Does not fit if:** your belief was accurate and you simply acted against it — that is
condition (i), and present bias or visceral factors fit better.

**Specimen sentence (≈35 words):**
> *My probability estimate was generated by the availability heuristic (Tversky & Kahneman,
> 1974): I substituted ease of recall for frequency, over-weighting a single vivid recent
> instance and producing a belief Bayes' rule would not sanction.*

## Future-application ideas (component D)

- **Consider the opposite** (Lord, Lepper & Preston, 1984): actively generate reasons the
  preferred conclusion is wrong. One of the few debiasing techniques with real evidence behind it.
- **Reference-class forecasting** (Kahneman & Tversky, 1979b): forecast from the base rate of
  comparable past cases ("outside view"), not from the details of this one ("inside view").
- **Pre-mortem** (Klein, 2007): assume the decision failed; write the post-mortem *before*
  committing. Surfaces risks optimism bias suppresses.
- **Anchor hygiene:** form your own valuation *before* seeing the asking price; write it down.
