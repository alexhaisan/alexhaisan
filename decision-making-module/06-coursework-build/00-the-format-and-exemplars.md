# 00 — The Submission Format & the Four Exemplars

> **Read this before anything else in this folder.** The lecturer supplied four example
> assessments used in a Lecture 2 activity. They settle what the submission actually looks
> like — which no amount of inference from the brief could.

---

## 1. The format — all four use it

```
Instrumental Rational Choice Application Exercise

Decision:            [the decision, one or two sentences]
Actions Available:   [the options]
Preferences:         [what I wanted / wanted to avoid]
Beliefs:             [what I thought each option would lead to]
Evidence:            [what I based those beliefs on]
Conclusion:          [IRC's prediction, what I did, and where the gap is]
```

### Three things to copy

1. **Labelled headings, not prose.** Every exemplar signposts each component by name. The
   marker never has to hunt. This also makes the word limit survivable.
2. **"Beliefs", not "Expectations".** The lecture slides say *Expectations*; Elster's book and
   all four exemplars say *Beliefs*. **Use Beliefs** — match the exemplars — and you can note
   the equivalence once.
3. **Combining Beliefs/Evidence is allowed.** Exemplars 3 and 4 use a single
   `Beliefs/Evidence:` heading. Lecture 2 slide 5 explicitly endorses this: *"Often the
   relationship between expectations and evidence is important, might be worth combining
   them."* At 300 words this is a real saving — take it if the two are entangled in your case.

### Length

| Exemplar | Body words (excl. title) |
|---|---|
| 1 — Grandmother's money | ~197 |
| 2 — iPhone | ~166 |
| 3 — Kebab | ~190 |
| 4 — Sober October | ~258 |

**Mean ≈ 203. None reaches 300.** The limit is a ceiling, not a target. Do not pad to hit it.
Aim for **230–270** — enough to go deeper than these do, with room to spare.

### A diagram is acceptable
Exemplar 1 ends *"This is represented by the purple arrow on the diagram below"* — it
included a copy of Elster's Figure 11.1 with the offending link highlighted. That is an
efficient way to show *where* the deviation sits. Check whether figure labels count toward
your 300.

## 2. The four exemplars, read critically

> ⚠️ These were used in a **lecture activity**, which usually means students were asked to
> evaluate them. Treat them as a mix of strong and flawed, not as model answers. My
> assessment of each follows — judge it yourself against the lecture guidance.

### Exemplar 1 — Grandmother's money ⭐⭐⭐⭐
*Inheritance: buy a car, or donate to charity. Chose charity.*

**The strength — and it's a real one.** This exemplar attacks the word ***instrumental***
rather than rationality in general:

> *"I was not instrumental, because I didn't base my decision just on outcomes — **the fact
> that I got the money from my grandmother affected how I decided to use the money**."*

That is precisely right, and it matches slide 14 of Lecture 1: *"individuals only look at the
outcome... ends always justify means (in fact, means don't matter at all)."* Money is
fungible; where it came from is a backward-looking fact with no bearing on which future
outcome is best. Letting the **source** of a resource drive its use is a clean violation of
the instrumental/consequentialist core.

**The weakness.** The component mapping is muddled. *"I believed that my grandmother would
have preferred for me to give the money to charity"* is filed under **Beliefs** — but
honouring her wishes is functioning as one of **his preferences**. If it's a preference, IRC
accommodates it and there's no deviation at all. The exemplar survives only because it
reframes the issue as non-instrumentality rather than irrationality.

**What to take:** the *non-instrumentality* move is excellent and under-used. **What to
avoid:** letting a preference sit in the Beliefs row.

---

### Exemplar 2 — The new iPhone ⭐⭐⭐
*Buy the new model, or keep the current phone.*

**The strength.** One sentence does almost all the work, and it's the cleanest single
deviation in the whole set:

> *"My belief that my phone was about to break is also an example of **wishful thinking — I
> wanted that to be true so I believed it to be so**."*

That is **the blocked arrow in Figure 11.1**, named and applied. Preferences contaminating
beliefs. Lecture 1 slide 27: *"Expectations should be formed without any impact from
preferences — no wishful thinking."*

**Two weaknesses.**
- **Preferences are mis-mapped.** *"The new iPhone has better camera quality, improved
  battery health and faster connection"* — those are **product attributes**, i.e. outcomes,
  not preferences. The preferences are *I value good photos / reliability / speed*. Lecture 2
  slide 4 asks: *"What do I want/value in this choice?"* — not what the product has.
- **The happiness argument is weak.** *"I believed the new model would give me much greater
  subjective happiness, even though I know there isn't much link between material wealth and
  happiness"* — this says the preference was *mistaken*, which Lecture 1 slide 25 rules out
  (*"preferences cannot be intrinsically irrational"*).
- **A missed opportunity:** *"Lots of my friends had already bought the new model, and this
  made me believe it was the best phone"* is a perfectly good Evidence→Beliefs failure
  (friends' purchases are weak evidence of quality) — and the conclusion never develops it.

**What to take:** name wishful thinking explicitly, as a mechanism. **What to avoid:**
listing product features as preferences; arguing your preference was wrong.

---

### Exemplar 3 — The kebab ⭐⭐⭐
*Bought a kebab on Tuesday with a fridge full of food bought Monday.*

**The strength.** The preference conflict is stated plainly and is genuinely three-way:

> *"I really like kebabs, but I don't like wasting food. I also don't like wasting money."*

Lecture 1 slide 25: preferences *"are often **contradictory**, and involve **trade-off** —
this is an important element of the analysis."* This exemplar does that better than the
others. It also sets up the counterfactual properly — *"I could then save the money and buy a
kebab in the future when my fridge is empty"* — showing the rational option wasn't
self-denial, just **rescheduling**. Nice.

**The weakness — and it's the instructive one.** The conclusion **describes without
diagnosing**:

> *"I chose to buy a kebab, **telling myself** that it was a good use of money because I
> would have leftovers the next day, and that I would definitely make sure I used all the
> food in the fridge."*

"Telling myself" is doing a lot of unacknowledged work. That is **wishful thinking** —
a belief ("I'll definitely use the food") formed from the desire to justify the kebab,
in direct contradiction of the evidence row ("several times I have had to throw away food").
It stops one sentence short of naming the component and the mechanism.

**What to take:** the explicit preference trade-off, and a counterfactual that's realistic
rather than ascetic. **What to avoid:** narrating the violation without labelling it.

---

### Exemplar 4 — Sober October ⭐⭐⭐⭐
*Participate and stop drinking for a month, or drink normally.*

**Analytically the most ambitious of the four.** It names *two* distinct violations.

**① Confirmation bias in belief-updating:**
> *"Having seen people drinking having a better time than those who do not I reinforce the
> idea enjoyment is linked to drinking however, this is co[n]firmation bias as I ignore the
> evidence of those individuals that enjoy themselves when sober and **do not update my
> beliefs accordingly. This is irrational.**"*

A proper Evidence→Beliefs failure, with the mechanism named.

**② Evidence feeding back into preferences:**
> *"The evidence of greater enjoyment when drinking then impacted my supposed immutable
> preferences going against what the model... dictates: preferences should not be affected by
> evidence or beliefs."*

This is the sharpest structural observation in the set. **In Figure 11.1 there is no arrow
pointing *into* Desires.** Preferences are an input to the model, not an output of it. If
your beliefs reshape what you want, the model's architecture is broken.

> ⚠️ **One caution if you borrow this.** The exemplar says preferences *"cannot be changed"*.
> Elster explicitly disagrees (p. 193): *"Nor do we need to require that desires be stable,
> not even in the minimal sense of excluding temporary preference changes."* The defensible
> version is narrower: preferences may change, but **not as a result of the beliefs and
> evidence inside this decision** — because the diagram has no such arrow. State it that way
> and it holds; state it the exemplar's way and the set reading contradicts you.

**The weakness: the writing.** *"In additions"*, *"allow to save"*, *"do not update by
beliefs"*, *"conformation bias"*. It's also the longest at ~258 words. Good analysis, poorly
executed — and since you have a proofreading pass planned, this is the easiest gap to close.

**What to take:** two named violations; the no-arrow-into-Preferences point. **What to
avoid:** submitting unproofread; overstating what the model requires.

## 3. What the four have in common — your checklist

| Every exemplar does this | Do it |
|---|---|
| Labelled headings per component | ☐ |
| One specific, recent, personal decision | ☐ |
| Exactly two options under *Actions Available* | ☐ |
| States IRC's prediction explicitly ("IRC would predict…") | ☐ |
| States what they actually did | ☐ |
| Ends on the gap | ☐ |

| The better ones also | Do it |
|---|---|
| State **conflicting** preferences and the trade-off (3, 4) | ☐ |
| **Name the component** where the deviation sits (4) | ☐ |
| **Name the mechanism** — wishful thinking, confirmation bias, non-instrumentality (1, 2, 4) | ☐ |
| Make the counterfactual realistic, not ascetic (3) | ☐ |

| None of them does | Your edge |
|---|---|
| Answer Lecture 2 slide 6's question: **which component has the *biggest* difference?** | ☐ |
| Cite Elster, or any source | ☐ |
| Use Elster's three optimality requirements to locate the failure | ☐ |

> The third column is where you can beat these. Slide 6 asks students to identify *"**which
> component has the biggest difference** between how a rational decision maker behaves and
> how the human decision-maker in this example behaved."* Singular. Naming one component as
> the primary site of deviation — and saying why it beats the others — is a stronger close
> than any of the four manage.
