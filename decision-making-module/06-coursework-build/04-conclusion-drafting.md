# 04 — Drafting the Conclusion

> The Conclusion is the only section that carries real analytical marks. The other five
> sections are *mapping* — they show you can apply the model. The Conclusion is where you
> show you understand what the model **claims**, and where your behaviour broke it.
>
> Everything here is built from the module's own materials. Nothing needs your decision
> except the final fill-in.

---

## 1. The four moves a strong Conclusion makes

| # | Move | Why |
|---|---|---|
| **1** | **State IRC's prediction** — "IRC would predict…" | All four exemplars do this. It's the counterfactual the task sentence demands: *"identifying how an instrumentally rational decision-maker with your preferences would act"* |
| **2** | **State what you actually did** | The task: *"noting how that differed from your decision"* |
| **3** | **Name THE component with the biggest gap** | L2 slide 6: *"**which component has the biggest difference**…?"* — **singular**. None of the four exemplars does this. |
| **4** | **Name the mechanism** | Not "I was irrational" but *wishful thinking* / *restricted option set* / *evidence over-weighted* / *not instrumental*. Exemplars 1, 2 and 4 do this; exemplar 3 doesn't, and it's the weakest. |

**Optional move 5 — one clause of evaluation.** Costs ~15 words, lifts the register:
> *"This does not make the model wrong: it is the benchmark that makes the gap measurable
> (Croson and Gächter, 2010, p. 123)."*

Use it only if you're under budget. Moves 1–4 come first.

---

## 2. Sentence patterns

Fill these in rather than starting from blank.

**Move 1 — the prediction**
> *"IRC would predict I choose ___, because my preference for ___ combined with my belief
> that ___ makes it the higher-expected-utility action."*

**Move 2 — what happened**
> *"I chose ___ instead."*

**Move 3 — the component**
> *"The largest divergence is at the **___** component."*
> *"The components where my behaviour matched IRC were ___; the gap sits almost entirely in ___."*

**Move 4 — the mechanism**
> *"___ , which is ___ : my ___ shaped my ___ rather than the other way round."*

**Optional — Elster's requirement**
> *"In Elster's terms, requirement (ii) failed: my belief was not the best supported by the
> evidence available to me (Elster, 2007, p. 191)."*

## 3. Four model Conclusions — one per component

> ⚠️ **These are illustrations, not submissions.** The scenarios are generic. Use the
> *shape*, supply your own decision. Each is ~80 words, which is the right weight given the
> exemplars run 166–258 words in total.

### Model A — the deviation is at **ACTION**
*(options unconsciously restricted — L1 slide 23, L2 slide 5 "restricted by framing")*

> IRC would predict I compare **all** available choices. My preferences and beliefs were
> accurate, and my evidence was adequate. The largest divergence is at the **Action**
> component: I framed this as a binary — ___ or ___ — when ___ and ___ were equally
> available and cheaper. A rational decision-maker has no "I didn't think of that" moments.
> The restriction was never conscious, which is precisely why it went unnoticed: my framing
> of the problem, not my reasoning within it, determined the outcome.

### Model B — the deviation is at **EXPECTATIONS**
*(wishful thinking — the blocked arrow; L1 slide 27)*

> IRC would predict I choose ___, since the evidence available supported ___. The gap is at
> the **Expectations** component. My belief that ___ was not formed from that evidence but
> from my preference for ___ — I wanted it to be true, so I believed it. Elster's model
> forbids this: preferences may reach beliefs only through evidence-gathering, never
> directly. As he puts it, the wish must not be "the father of the thought" (2007, p. 205).

### Model C — the deviation is at **EVIDENCE**
*(over- or under-valued — L1 slide 29)*

> IRC would predict I weight evidence by how informative it is. My preferences were stable
> and my option set complete. The largest divergence is at the **Evidence** component: I
> treated ___ as strong evidence of ___ when it carries almost none, while ignoring ___,
> which I already had and which pointed the other way. The failure is not that my search was
> too short — it is that I valued the evidence I collected in proportion to how welcome it
> was.

### Model D — the deviation is in being **INSTRUMENTAL** at all
*(like exemplar 1 — the strongest conceptual move in the set)*

> IRC would predict I choose ___, which best serves my preferences. I chose ___. The gap is
> not in any single component but in the model's **instrumental** character: IRC evaluates
> actions purely by their outcomes, so ___ — a fact about the past, not about any future
> consequence — should have been irrelevant. It was decisive. My decision was rational in the
> ordinary sense but not instrumentally rational, because the means mattered to me
> independently of the ends.

## 4. The Preferences trap — read before using Model B, C or D

L2 slide 5: *"**'I went against my preferences' is not a compelling argument**"* and
*"avoid privileging some preferences over others."*

| ❌ Don't write | ✅ Write instead |
|---|---|
| "I went against my preference to save money" | "My preferences for ___ and ___ conflicted; the trade-off was resolved by ___" |
| "I really wanted X but gave in to Y" | "I wanted both X and Y. IRC requires the trade-off to be made on expected utility; mine was made on ___" |
| "My preference was irrational" | *(never — L1 slide 25: preferences cannot be intrinsically irrational)* |

**And the one that catches people out:** being tired, stressed, hungry or emotional is **not**
a deviation. Elster (2007, p. 193): an agent *"under the influence of emotion or drugs…
acts rationally."* If that's your story, the failure has to be relocated to Action,
Expectations or Evidence.

## 5. The full draft skeleton

Copy this into Word. Verdana 11pt, 1.5 spacing, 2.5cm margins.

```
Instrumental Rational Choice Application Exercise

Decision:            [1–2 sentences. Specific, recent, real.]              ~25 w

Actions Available:   [the options — and note any you didn't consider]      ~25 w

Preferences:         [flat list. What I wanted AND what I wanted to        ~40 w
                      avoid (L2 slide 4). Show the conflict.]

Beliefs/Evidence:    [what I expected each option to lead to, and what     ~60 w
                      I based that on. Combining these is sanctioned
                      by L2 slide 5.]

Conclusion:          [the four moves from §1 above]                        ~80 w

                                                            TOTAL ≈ 230 w
```

Leaves ~70 words of headroom under the 300 limit — use it to deepen the Conclusion, not to
pad the mapping.

## 6. Do you need a citation?

**None of the four exemplars cites anything.** So it is clearly not required for the 300-word
piece — the literature instruction belongs to the December essay.

But a parenthetical citation costs about **three words** and can only help:

> *"…the wish must not be 'the father of the thought' (Elster, 2007, p. 205)."*

If you include one, add a one-line reference at the end (check whether it counts toward the
300 — see `../00-assessment/02-format-and-submission.md`). Verified, ready-to-use quotes with
locators are in `../07-references/03-quote-ledger.md`.

## 7. Final check on the Conclusion

- [ ] Does it say "IRC would predict…" explicitly?
- [ ] Does it say what I actually did?
- [ ] Does it name **one** component as the biggest gap?
- [ ] Does it name a **mechanism**, not just assert irrationality?
- [ ] Have I avoided "I went against my preferences"?
- [ ] Have I avoided "it turned out badly" and "I regret it now"?
- [ ] Would a marker know, from the last sentence alone, what broke?
