## Draft v1 — 22 October · 251 words

> **Instrumental Rational Choice Application Exercise**
>
> **Decision:** On release day I bought an iPhone 17 Pro for £1,099. My old phone still
> worked. Its battery did not hold charge.
>
> **Actions Available:** I weighed four options: an aftermarket battery replacement at £150,
> waiting for the iPhone 16 Pro to fall in price, the standard iPhone 17, or the Pro. A
> cheaper Samsung flagship I ruled out, since switching operating systems carries real costs.
> One option never entered the set: an official Apple battery repair, which is neither
> aftermarket nor second-hand.
>
> **Preferences:** I wanted a phone that worked, and the battery was the reason I was
> thinking about it at all. I also wanted the standing that comes with owning the newest
> model. I wanted to avoid slowness, and to avoid parts of unknown provenance.
>
> **Beliefs/Evidence:** I expected the Pro to be fast and dependable, and it is. I also
> expected the novelty to fade inside a week, because it has faded inside a week every
> previous time. My evidence came from keynote presentations, pre-release rumour and
> benchmark reviews. All of it concerned what the phone could do. None of it concerned how
> long I would care.
>
> **Conclusion:** An instrumentally rational agent with these preferences, status included,
> discounts a benefit expected to last a week and repairs the battery. I bought the Pro. The
> largest gap sits at Evidence: the decisive evidence was my own history with every previous
> phone, and I let keynote footage and benchmark reviews crowd it out. Wanting the phone
> settled which question I researched. The search could only return the answer I already
> wanted.

## Why it is built this way

### The strongest thing in Alex's material

> *"the novelty of having the new phone would have only lasted the first week… as all phones
> you become used to it and have it replace the next benchmark or expectation."*

**He knew in advance.** The belief was accurate and held at the time of choosing. That rules
out the easy answers and forces a better one:

| Not available as the deviation | Why |
|---|---|
| Wishful thinking | He did **not** believe the novelty would last. He said the opposite. |
| A mistaken belief | His expectations were correct on both counts: daily use, one-week novelty. |
| "I wanted clout, which is irrational" | L1 slide 25 — preferences cannot be intrinsically irrational. Status is admissible. |
| "I went against my preferences" | L2 slide 5 — not a compelling argument. |
| The Samsung rejection | Ecosystem lock-in and OS switching costs are **real constraints**; peer norms are a **preference**. Neither is a deviation. |

### So the failure is Elster's requirement (i)

> *"the action must be optimal, **given the beliefs**"* — Elster (2007, p. 191)

Belief accurate. Search adequate in volume. Action did not follow. That is a precise,
defensible diagnosis, and it is rarer than the wishful-thinking story most students will tell.

### The biggest gap: Evidence

L1 slide 29: *"Violations often noticeable through impact on expectations — evidence is
**over- or under-valued**."*

He held two bodies of evidence:

| Evidence | Volume | Bearing on the decision |
|---|---|---|
| Keynotes, rumour, benchmark reviews | Enormous | What the phone **can do** |
| His own history with previous phones | One data point per phone | **How long he would value it** |

The second was decisive and he under-weighted it. That is the gap.

### The interaction (marking criterion 2)

> *"Wanting the phone settled which question I researched."*

Elster permits desires to reach beliefs **through** evidence-gathering — desires as the
*grandfather* of beliefs (2007, p. 205 n. 12). But here the desire determined **which question
was asked at all**. The search was pointed at capability, never at valuation, so it could not
have returned a disconfirming answer.

That is a relationship between Preferences and Evidence, stated in one sentence. It is what
L3 slide 17 asks for.

## Three things to confirm before this is final

1. **What did you actually pay?** Use the receipt, not my figure. £1,099 was the UK launch
   price for the 256GB model, but the sources were poor and storage tier changes it.
2. **Which phone did you upgrade from?** This decides whether an official Apple battery
   repair was actually available for it, and at what price. If it was not, the
   *Actions Available* line needs rewording.
3. **Did "repair the battery" mean "aftermarket" in your head from the start?** If you
   considered the official route and rejected it for a reason, that reason belongs in the
   draft and the Action point changes.

## The alternative framing, if you prefer it

Lead on **Action** rather than Evidence: you collapsed "repair the battery" into "aftermarket
battery", which excluded the one option satisfying both your stated ends — a working phone,
and no parts of unknown provenance — at roughly a tenth of the cost.

Logically it is the cleaner argument, and it maps onto L3 slide 16 exactly: *"decisions where
you have restricted choices too early… where you didn't consider a good potential choice."*

It depends entirely on question 2 above. Answer that and we choose.
