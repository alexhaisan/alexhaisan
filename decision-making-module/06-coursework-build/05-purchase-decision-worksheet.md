# 05 — Worksheet: a Purchase or Subscription Decision

> You've picked a purchase/subscription decision. This narrows things usefully — it's also
> the lecturer's own territory (L2 slide 20 lists *"Subscription Products"* and *"Product
> Pricing/Menus"*; slide 18 is consumer behaviour).
>
> Answer §1, and the draft writes itself from §3.

---

## 1. Six questions — answer these and we're done

| # | Question | Your answer |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **What did you buy / keep paying for / not cancel?** When? Roughly what did it cost? | |
| 2 | **What options did you actually consider?** Usually two. | |
| 3 | **What options existed that you *didn't* consider?** Downgrade? Pause? Wait? Buy used? Cancel and re-subscribe later? | |
| 4 | **What did you want from it?** And — L2 slide 4 — **what did you want to avoid?** | |
| 5 | **What did you expect it to give you?** How often did you think you'd use it? | |
| 6 | **What did you base that on?** Own past use? A review? Friends? A discount? The price itself? | |

> Q3 is the one people skip and it's usually where the deviation lives.

---

## 2. How purchase decisions usually break

Three patterns. Find yours — then the Conclusion is a fill-in.

### Pattern A — the option set was narrower than reality
**Component: ACTION.** You framed it as *buy / don't buy* or *renew / cancel*, when
downgrade, pause, delay or second-hand were all available. L1 slide 23: *"Violations at this
step often involve **unconscious restriction of available possible choices**."*

### Pattern B — the price or the discount was treated as evidence
**Component: EVIDENCE → EXPECTATIONS.** A "70% off" tag is information about the seller's
pricing, not about whether the thing is worth having to you. Same for *"my friends all
bought it"* (exemplar 2 spots this and then fails to use it). L1 slide 29: evidence
*"over- or under-valued"*.

### Pattern C — you believed you'd use it more than your own record supported
**Component: EXPECTATIONS.** The classic gym-membership shape. You had the evidence — your
own past usage — and formed a belief from what you wanted instead. This is **wishful
thinking**, the blocked arrow. L1 slide 27.

> Most real purchases are **A + C** together. That's fine — but L2 slide 6 asks which gap is
> **biggest**. Pick one to lead on, mention the other in a clause.

## 3. Two complete worked drafts

> ⚠️ **Illustrations, not submissions.** Invented scenarios, written to the exemplar format
> and length. Use the shape; supply your own facts.

### Worked draft A — a subscription renewal *(≈235 words)*

> **Instrumental Rational Choice Application Exercise**
>
> **Decision:** On 3 October I let a £14.99/month streaming subscription renew, having opened
> the cancellation page and then closed it.
>
> **Actions Available:** I treated this as a choice between renewing and cancelling. Two
> further options existed that I did not consider: downgrading to the £5.99 ad-supported
> tier, and using the three-month pause the account settings offer.
>
> **Preferences:** I wanted to reduce my monthly outgoings. I also wanted to keep access to
> two series I was partway through, and to avoid the mild irritation of re-subscribing later
> if I changed my mind.
>
> **Beliefs/Evidence:** I believed I would watch more over winter than I had over summer. The
> evidence available was my own viewing history — four hours across the previous four months
> — which did not support that belief, and a vague sense that "there's always something on",
> which came from the platform's recommendations rather than from my use of them.
>
> **Conclusion:** IRC would predict I choose the option with the highest expected utility
> given those preferences. Valuing my actual use at roughly £4 a month against a £15 cost,
> that is pausing or downgrading — not renewing. The largest divergence is at the **Action**
> component: by framing a binary, I excluded the two options that best served my own
> preferences, and no comparison of expected utilities could recover them. A rational
> decision-maker has no "I didn't think of that" moments.

---

### Worked draft B — a discounted purchase *(≈240 words)*

> **Instrumental Rational Choice Application Exercise**
>
> **Decision:** On 11 October I bought a £60 pair of running shoes reduced from £130, having
> gone online to buy a replacement charger.
>
> **Actions Available:** Buy the shoes, or not. I did not consider the option I had arrived
> with — buying only the charger — as a live alternative once the sale banner appeared.
>
> **Preferences:** I wanted to run more regularly, to spend less this month, and to avoid the
> feeling of missing a good deal.
>
> **Beliefs/Evidence:** I believed the shoes were worth £130 and that I was therefore £70
> better off. My evidence for that valuation was the crossed-out price on the listing. I
> already owned running shoes bought fourteen months ago with limited use, which was better
> evidence of how much I would run, and which I did not weigh.
>
> **Conclusion:** IRC would predict I buy only if the shoes' expected value to me exceeds
> £60, assessed on evidence about my own running. The largest divergence is at the
> **Evidence** component: I treated the seller's reference price as evidence of the shoes'
> value to me, when it is information about the seller's pricing strategy and nothing else,
> while discounting the stronger evidence of my existing unused pair. My expectation of
> running more was then shaped by the purchase rather than preceding it — the evidence I
> valued most was the evidence that was most welcome.

## 4. What makes these score

| Feature | Where |
|---|---|
| Specific date and figure | Decision line |
| **Unconsidered options named** | Actions Available — none of the four exemplars does this |
| Preferences listed flat, including what's **avoided** (L2 slide 4) | Preferences |
| Evidence separated from what was **concluded** from it | Beliefs/Evidence |
| "IRC would predict…" stated explicitly | Conclusion, move 1 |
| **One** component named as the biggest gap (L2 slide 6) | Conclusion, move 3 |
| Mechanism named, not just "irrational" | Conclusion, move 4 |
| Neither says "I went against my preferences" | throughout |
| Neither says "it turned out badly" | throughout |

Both sit ~235–240 words — comfortably inside 300, and longer than three of the four
exemplars.
