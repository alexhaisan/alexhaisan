## 🚨 The two warnings from Lecture 2, slide 5

Before you screen anything, internalise these. They disqualify most first attempts.

### 1. *"'I went against my preferences' is not a compelling argument"*

You hold **conflicting** preferences, so **every** choice goes against one of them. Saying
you acted against a preference describes a trade-off, not a failure. Exemplar 3 wanted a
kebab *and* not to waste food — whichever he chose, one preference lost.

The deviation must be in **how** the trade-off was made, or in the **Action**,
**Expectations** or **Evidence** components. Never in the bare fact that a preference lost.

### 2. *"Avoid privileging some preferences over others"*

Don't quietly promote one preference to "the real one" and demote the rest to temptation.
"I *really* wanted to save money but gave in to wanting a kebab" smuggles in a ranking the
model doesn't give you. **List them flat**, then show the trade-off.

---

## Why this is the highest-leverage choice

The brief asks for a decision *"which does not follow this model."* Three-quarters of the
marks (components B, C, D) flow from that single choice:

- **B** needs a decision with a **clean, statable IRC counterfactual**
- **C** needs a decision whose mechanism matches a **theory taught on this module**
- **D** needs a decision where the fix is **specific and implementable**

A decision that is genuinely a *preference* fails all three at once.

## Step 2 — Screen each candidate

Fill one block per candidate. Use `../01-core-theory/04-irc-worked-template.md` for the
full version on the top two or three.

```
CANDIDATE #__
Decision (one sentence):
Date (must be recent):

My preference / the end I was pursuing:
What an IRC agent WITH THAT PREFERENCE would have done:
What I actually did:
The gap:

Deviation Test — tick at least one:
  [ ] Inconsistency (named axiom: ______)
  [ ] Belief failure (how: ______)
  [ ] Means–ends failure (how: ______)
  [ ] Search failure (how: ______)

Elster condition(s) violated:  (i) / (ii) / (iii)
WHICH COMPONENT HAS THE BIGGEST GAP?  (L2 slide 6 — answer in one sentence)
```

> That last line is the one none of the four exemplars answers. Lecture 2 slide 6 asks
> *"**which component has the biggest difference** between how a rational decision maker
> behaves and how the human decision-maker in this example behaved?"* — singular. Build your
> conclusion on it.

## Step 3 — Score and choose

Score each candidate 1–5. **Highest total wins — but any score of 1 on criteria 1–3 is
disqualifying.**

| # | Criterion | What a 5 looks like |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Genuine deviation** (not a preference) | At least one Deviation Test box ticked with confidence; the counterfactual is airtight |
| 2 | **Clean counterfactual** | You can state in one sentence exactly what an IRC agent would have done, and why |
| 3 | **Module theory fits** | A theory taught on this module explains the *mechanism*, not just the label |
| 4 | **Concisely describable** | The whole situation fits in ~40 words. Complex decisions eat the word budget |
| 5 | **Non-trivial but safe to disclose** | Real enough to be interesting; nothing you'd rather a marker didn't read |
| 6 | **Specific fix available** | Component D writes itself, and is architectural rather than motivational |

## Decisions that reliably score well

Because the counterfactual is airtight and the theory is unambiguous:

| Decision type | Why it works | Theory |
|---|---|---|
| **Didn't cancel an unused subscription** | Status quo clearly suboptimal by your own valuation; cancelling was free | Status quo bias / loss aversion |
| **Finished a course/book/series you weren't enjoying** | Pure sunk cost; forward-looking value was negative | Sunk cost fallacy |
| **Bought something because it was heavily discounted** | Transaction utility distinct from acquisition utility | Mental accounting |
| **Broke a plan you'd made a week earlier, with no new information** | Self-evidencing preference reversal | Present bias / hyperbolic discounting |
| **Never switched energy/bank/tariff despite known savings** | Inertia against a known, quantified gain | Status quo bias / default effect |
| **Chose based on one vivid story rather than base rates** | Clean belief failure | Availability heuristic |
| **Paid a premium to eliminate a tiny risk entirely** | Certainty effect | Allais / probability weighting |

## Decisions that reliably score badly

| Decision type | Why it fails |
|---|---|
| "I spent money on something enjoyable" | A preference. Admissible under IRC. |
| "I took the safe option" | Risk aversion is standard EUT. |
| "It turned out badly" | **Outcome ≠ process.** The most common failure in this assessment. |
| "I didn't have enough information" | A constraint, unless search itself was non-optimal |
| "I helped someone at a cost to myself" | Other-regarding preferences are admissible — Elster (2007, p. 193): *"the confusion of rationality and egoism is a crude error"* |
| **"I went against my preferences"** | **L2 slide 5: *"not a compelling argument"*** |
| "I was angry/tired/drunk and wanted something different" | Elster (2007, p. 193): such an agent *"acts rationally"*. Temporary preference change is allowed. |
| Anything vague or habitual ("I procrastinate") | Not a decision. Needs to be one identifiable choice. |
