# 07 — Prose and Style

> British English. Human rhythm. No repeated phrasing. This file is the specification for
> **how the thing reads**, which is separate from whether the analysis is right.

---

## 1. The constraint shapes the craft

This is not a flowing essay. It is six labelled sections, five of which are compressed
statements of fact, and one of which carries the argument. So the prose work is concentrated:

| Section | Register |
|---|---|
| Decision | Plain report. One or two sentences. No throat-clearing. |
| Actions Available | Near-list. Terse is correct here. |
| Preferences | Flat list. Resist the urge to rank or justify. |
| Beliefs / Evidence | Two linked statements: what I thought, what that rested on. |
| **Conclusion** | **The only place with real prose. All the rhythm work happens here.** |

Trying to make the mapping sections "well written" usually makes them worse. They should be
efficient. Save the writing for the Conclusion.

---

## 2. Sentence rhythm

Human writing varies. Machine writing clusters. The giveaway is not any single sentence, it
is that **every** sentence lands between fifteen and twenty-five words.

### The target
In a Conclusion of roughly 80 words you have four or five sentences. Aim for a spread like:

```
long (25-32)  ·  very short (2-6)  ·  long (25-35)  ·  short (6-10)
```

The short sentence after a long one is what creates the sense of a person thinking. Used
once, it lands. Used three times, it becomes a tic.

### Worked contrast — the same content, twice

**Monotone (and full of tells):**
> IRC would predict that I would choose to cancel the subscription, as this would align with
> my stated preferences. However, I chose to renew it instead, highlighting a significant
> deviation from the model. The largest divergence can be seen in the Action component,
> underscoring how framing restricted my available choices. This demonstrates that my
> decision-making process was not instrumentally rational.

Sentence lengths: 18 / 15 / 16 / 12. Nothing varies. And the tells are everywhere:
*align with*, *highlighting*, *significant*, *underscoring*, *demonstrates*, plus two
participle phrases bolted on to manufacture depth.

**Varied:**
> An instrumentally rational agent with these preferences cancels, or drops to the cheaper
> tier. I renewed. The gap sits at Action: I had framed a two-way choice when four options
> were live, and no comparison of expected utilities can recover an option that was never in
> the set. My beliefs were accurate and my evidence was sufficient. The failure came earlier
> than either.

Sentence lengths: 14 / 2 / 31 / 9 / 6. Same claims, 62 words, and it reads like someone
worked it out.

> Note what the two-word sentence does. It is the hinge of the whole piece, and giving it a
> sentence of its own is the cheapest emphasis available.

### Test it
Count the words in each sentence and write the numbers down. If the spread is narrower than
about 5 to 30, go back and break one up or join two.

## 3. British English

| Use | Not |
|---|---|
| behaviour, favour, labour | behavior, favor, labor |
| maximise, minimise, rationalise, prioritise, recognise | maximize, minimize, etc. |
| analyse, catalyse | analyze, catalyze |
| centre, theatre | center, theater |
| licence (noun) / license (verb) · practice (noun) / practise (verb) | — |
| judgement *(common in UK usage; "judgment" is standard in legal writing)* | — |
| programme *(except a computer program)* | program |
| towards, afterwards | toward, afterward |
| per cent *(two words)* or % | percent |
| learnt, spelt, dreamt *(acceptable; -ed forms also fine, just be consistent)* | — |
| 26 October 2026 | October 26, 2026 |

### Points that actually come up in this piece
- **Single quotation marks** for quoted phrases in Harvard/Cite Them Right; double inside single.
- **Punctuation outside the quotation marks** for a partial quote: *the wish must not be
  'the father of the thought'.*
- **Quoted American sources keep their spelling.** Kosfeld et al. wrote *behaviour* (Nature,
  British), but Xiao and Houser wrote *behavior*. Quote verbatim either way.
- **£ not $** in your own examples. Quote source figures in their original currency.
- **-ise is right for Nottingham.** Oxford University Press uses -ize; your institution
  does not. Pick -ise and never mix.

## 4. Repetition — the rule everyone gets backwards

Generic advice says vary your word choice. **For this piece that advice is actively wrong in
one direction and right in the other.**

### Technical terms must repeat exactly
**Action, Preferences, Beliefs, Expectations, Evidence, instrumental, expected utility.**

These are the model's vocabulary. Calling Preferences "desires" in one line and "wants" in
the next does not look varied; it looks like you are unsure which the component is called.
Worse, it triggers criterion 1 on the mark scheme: *"a lack of understanding of the
component."* **Name each component the same way every time.**

> This is **elegant variation**, and it is its own tell: *"The protagonist faces challenges.
> The main character overcomes obstacles. The central figure triumphs."* Nobody writes that
> on purpose.

### Connective tissue should vary
The glue is where repetition grates. Rotate naturally:

| Instead of using one word four times | Rotate |
|---|---|
| deviation | gap · divergence · departure · difference |
| shows | indicates · suggests · points to · means |
| because | since · as · given that |
| however | but · yet · though |

### The specific trap at 300 words
*"Decision"* and *"rational"* will each want to appear six or seven times. Some of that is
unavoidable and fine. But watch for the stacked version: *"my decision-making process in
this decision was not a rational decision."* Read aloud and you will hear it.

## 5. AI tells a marker will notice

They will read a lot of these. Some patterns have become unmistakable.

### Vocabulary to avoid entirely
delve · underscore · highlight *(as a verb)* · showcase · align with · crucial · pivotal ·
key *(as an adjective)* · landscape *(figurative)* · testament · foster · navigate
*(figurative)* · intricate · robust *(unless statistical)* · leverage *(as a verb)*

### Constructions to avoid
| Pattern | Example | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Participle bolt-on** | *"…restricted my choices, **highlighting** how framing operates."* | Cut it, or make it its own sentence with a subject |
| **Negative parallelism** | *"It is not merely X; it is Y."* | Say Y |
| **Forced rule of three** | *"preferences, beliefs and expectations"* when only two are relevant | Name the two |
| **Copula avoidance** | *"This **serves as** an example of…"* | *"This is…"* |
| **Significance inflation** | *"This **marks a pivotal moment** in my understanding"* | Delete |
| **Filler openers** | *"It is important to note that…"* · *"In order to…"* | *"Note that"* · *"To"* |
| **Stacked hedges** | *"may potentially suggest that it could"* | Pick one hedge, or none |
| **Em dashes** | *"The gap — and it is a real one — sits at Action"* | Use commas, a colon, or two sentences |

> On em dashes specifically: they are the single most reliable signal at the moment. There is
> no sentence in a 300-word piece that needs one. Colons and semicolons do the job and look
> deliberate.

### Formatting tells
- Bold scattered through the body text. Your headings are already labelled; that is enough.
- Perfectly equal section lengths.
- Curly quotes pasted in from elsewhere.
- Any emoji. Obviously.

### What **cannot** be avoided, and shouldn't be
Hyphenation in **decision-making** is correct and standard in this field; it is in the module
title. Keep it. Generic de-hyphenating advice does not apply to a term of art.

## 6. Register — first person, plain, confident

The lecturer says it is **not an essay**, and all four exemplars are written in plain first
person. So:

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| *"I renewed."* | *"The decision was made to renew."* |
| *"I had framed a two-way choice."* | *"A two-way framing was adopted."* |
| State the finding | Apologise for it |
| *"My belief was not supported by the evidence I held."* | *"One might argue my belief was perhaps insufficiently supported."* |

**Self-criticism is the content here, so write it without flinching.** Exemplar 4's best
line is *"This is irrational"* — four words, no hedge. Hedging your own analysis reads as
lack of conviction, and at 300 words you cannot afford the qualifiers.

What you should **not** do is editorialise about how the experience felt. *"I was shocked to
realise…"* adds nothing a marker can credit. Emotion belongs in the content when the emotion
*is* the mechanism (a preference that shifted under pressure), not as decoration.

## 7. The read-aloud pass

Last thing before formatting. Read the whole piece out loud, slowly.

- **Breath test.** Any sentence you cannot finish in one breath is too long, or needs a comma.
- **Stumble test.** Anywhere your tongue trips is a clause that needs reordering.
- **Echo test.** If a word sounds familiar, you have used it recently. Check.
- **Flat test.** If four sentences in a row have the same shape, break one.

Then run it through Copilot for spelling and grammar only. **Do not let a proofreading tool
rewrite your sentences** — it will smooth exactly the variation you built in, and the result
will read like everything else in the pile. Accept corrections; reject "improvements".

Log that use in [`../00-assessment/03-ai-use-log.md`](../00-assessment/03-ai-use-log.md).

## 8. One-page checklist

- [ ] Sentence lengths in the Conclusion range at least 5–30 words
- [ ] At least one very short sentence, placed deliberately
- [ ] Component names used identically every time
- [ ] Connectives varied
- [ ] No em dashes
- [ ] No words from the avoid list
- [ ] No participle phrases bolted onto sentence ends
- [ ] -ise spellings throughout, consistently
- [ ] £ in my own figures; source currencies quoted as published
- [ ] First person, active voice, no hedging of my own conclusions
- [ ] Read aloud end to end
- [ ] Proofed for errors only, not restyled
