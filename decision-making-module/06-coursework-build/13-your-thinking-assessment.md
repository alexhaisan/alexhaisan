# 13 — Your Thinking: good, weak, missing

> Plain assessment of **how you actually decided**, not of the write-up.

---

## What was good

**1. You were honest about wanting the money and the status.**
Most people bury that and claim they were weighing career fit. You named it. The model
doesn't treat wanting money as a fault, so naming it costs you nothing and makes everything
else you say more believable.

**2. You spotted that specialising at 21 closes doors.**
A niche hedge fund role is narrower than a chartered qualification. That is genuine
forward-looking reasoning and it is the strongest thing in your actual decision.

**3. You thought about the downside, not just the upside.**
Quit Marshall Wace after a year and you have no job and no qualification. Most people
offered £120,000 do not run that branch at all.

**4. You knew the qualification travels and the job doesn't.**
The ACA works anywhere. The specific role works at one kind of firm. That asymmetry is real
and you had it.

---

## What was weak

**1. You stopped at the headline.**
£120,000 against £38,000 felt like £82,000. It is £37,898. More than half the difference
disappears into tax, National Insurance and the student loan, and the £40,000 bonus is worth
£15,600 once it crosses £100,000. You are training as an accountant. The sums were twenty
minutes.

**2. You counted money that was already gone.**
Three years of tuition is spent whether you take the job or not. It cannot make one option
better than the other, because it lands on both equally. Treating it as a reason to stay is
the clearest single break from the model in the whole decision.

**3. You closed the question too early.**
Two options, when there were three. You never asked whether the firm would wait eight months.

**4. You turned an assumption into a fact.**
"They won't defer" was a guess. The one person who could have answered was on the phone with
you, and you were already talking to them. That is the part that is hardest to defend, and
it is also the part that makes the piece interesting.

---

## The thing worth understanding

**The decision was probably right. The reasoning was not.** Those are separate, and the
model cares about the second.

Elster's point is that the action has to *follow from* the beliefs that justify it. Reaching
a good outcome by accident is not the same as reasoning well. Your reasons were a figure that
was wrong by half, money that was already spent, and an option you hadn't thought of. The
reasons that would have justified withdrawing — the qualification travels, the hours are
brutal, the real gap is modest, you're 21 — you arrived at afterwards, with me.

That is a more interesting thing to write about than "I was irrational and chose badly", and
it is harder to spot.

### Why this isn't just judging yourself with hindsight

Because nothing you'd need was hidden. The tax figures were public. The deferral question
needed one sentence on a call you were already on. The qualification's portability you
already knew. None of it arrived later. You just didn't go and get it.

That is the difference between *couldn't have known* and *didn't look*, and only the second
is a failure of the model.

## What's missing for high marks

**1. Show how the parts connect, not just what they were.**
Listing four components gets you a solid mark. The marks above that come from showing one
feeding into another: wanting the job meant you only looked at the headline, which meant the
figure you believed was wrong, which meant the decision rested on it. Figure 1 does this
visually so the prose doesn't have to spend forty words on it.

**2. Say something about where the model itself falls short.**
Neither of the two sample answers you showed me does this well. One asserts it in passing,
the other not at all. The honest version here: the model tells you to gather the right amount
of information, but gives you no way to know what the right amount is until you've gathered
it. You didn't do the sums because nothing told you they would change the answer, and only
doing them reveals that they do.

**3. The AI appendix.**
A full third of the rubric and still blank. It asks for evidence that you understood and
corrected what came out. You changed the scenario, corrected a factual error about the option
set, and pushed back on the writing. That is exactly what it wants, and nobody else will have
a record like it.
